Institutions and economic development: the persistence of history

Today’s question

Can a colonial labor system abolished in 1812 still make Peruvian children shorter today?

  • Why are some countries so much richer than others?
  • Can decisions made centuries ago still shape lives now?
  • If so, how — what carries history forward?

Where we’re headed

  • The development puzzle: enormous gaps in income across countries
  • The institutions hypothesis (North) — and why it was hard to test
  • Dell (2010) on Peru’s mining mita: history frozen at a boundary, and why its effects persist

The development puzzle

Incomes differ enormously across countries

Source: Our World in Data (visualization); Maddison Project Database (2018)

A tenfold gap

  • U.S. and Western Europe: roughly $50,000+ per person
  • Parts of Sub-Saharan Africa: about ten times lower

Development economics

  • The field asking how to improve outcomes in developing countries
  • Not just the same research in poorer places — different technologies (e.g., tax enforcement) and different institutions change which questions and policies make sense
  • Two broad approaches in modern work:
    1. Policy evaluation — test specific programs (often with RCTs)
    2. Institutional design — improve government and basic institutions

Why study institutions?

  • Critique of pure policy evaluation: you can’t test and fix every policy a country will ever need
  • Alternative: improve institutions so countries solve problems themselves — sanitation, public health, schools
  • More scalable — but much harder to study and to change

The institutions hypothesis

  • Douglass North (Nobel laureate): strong institutions — property rights, political structures — are central to development
  • Not natural resources, not climate: the productive, high-income countries had better rules of the game
  • Bold claim — but how would you ever test it?

The problem with cross-country tests

  • Early evidence: compare rich vs. poor countries
  • But countries differ in geography, culture, history, policy — all at once
  • Impossible to isolate the causal role of institutions
  • Modern approach: sub-national differences in institutions + historical data recovered from archives + modern causal methods

Peru’s mining mita

Today’s reading: Dell (2010)

  • “The Persistent Effects of Peru’s Mining Mita,” Econometrica
  • Melissa Dell pioneered using historical data analysis to estimate causal effects of institutions
  • Setting: a forced-labor institution from Spanish colonial Peru

What was the mita?

  • Instituted by the Spanish government in 1573; abolished in 1812
  • Communities near the Potosí (silver) and Huancavelica (mercury) mines had to send one-seventh of their adult men each year to work in the mines
  • One of the largest and longest-running forced-labor systems in the Americas

A boundary drawn around the mines

The boundary used in the study

Inside vs. outside the line

  • Workers couldn’t commute long distances, so Spain drew a boundary around the mines
  • Inside the line: subject to the mita. Just outside: exempt
  • A sharp break in institutions — with villages sitting right on either side

Why this is a natural experiment

  • Compare places just inside vs. just outside the boundary
  • Very close to the line, villages share climate, elevation, crops, culture — everything except the mita
  • Any jump in outcomes at the line is plausibly the causal effect of the institution

Method spotlight: spatial regression discontinuity

  • Ordinary RD: treatment flips at a cutoff score (like the class-size rule in Project 2)
  • Spatial RD: treatment flips at a line on the map
  • Identification assumption: nothing else relevant changes sharply at the boundary
  • Estimate: the discontinuity in outcomes right at the line

The geography trap

  • Danger: parts of the boundary follow mountains — elevation and economies change right at the line
  • That would break the design: the jump could reflect geography, not the mita
  • Dell restricts to boundary segments where geographic conditions are smooth — similar land on both sides before 1573

Method spotlight: historical data analysis

  • The other half of the method: recovering data from archives
  • Dell collects records on district capitals, land tenure, haciendas (1689!), literacy (1876) — then links them to modern household surveys (heights, consumption)
  • Old institutions + modern causal techniques = questions we could never randomize

TopHat: Make a prediction

The mita ended in 1812 — over 200 years ago. Compared to villages just outside the old boundary, villages just inside today are…

  • A: Richer — the mines brought jobs and infrastructure
  • B: Poorer — the mita left lasting damage
  • C: The same — 200 years is plenty of time to converge

Result: consumption is lower inside the boundary

Two centuries later, the line is still in the data

  • Household consumption in 2001 drops sharply crossing into former mita districts — roughly 25% lower (Dell 2010)
  • Two centuries after abolition, living standards still jump at a line drawn in 1573

Result: children are more likely to be stunted

Reading the stunting map

  • Stunting: height more than two standard deviations below the age-expected level — a standard nutrition measure where governments track kids’ heights systematically
  • Crossing out of the mita region, the stunting rate falls by about 30% (≈ 6 percentage points)
  • History is literally visible in children’s bodies

Why does history persist?

Channel 1: property rights and land tenure

  • Non-mita areas developed a clear system of land ownership and property rights — set up historically, persisting to today
  • Mita areas never did — and no clear system emerged even after the mita ended
  • Echoes North: property rights are the foundation everything else is built on

The haciendas in 1689

The haciendas in 1940

Why haciendas matter

  • Haciendas = large rural estates with secure land titles
  • Far more haciendas just outside the boundary — already visible in 1689, still true in 1940
  • Haciendas provided steady employment — and their owners had political muscle

Channel 2: literacy in 1876

Channel 2: roads in 2006

Public goods followed the landowners

  • Large landowners advocated for roads, schools, and other public goods in their areas
  • Literacy in 1876: lower inside the boundary
  • Road density in 2006: lower inside the boundary
  • Telling detail: roads outside the boundary run right past the large estates — built where owners pushed for them

Channel 3: markets and productivity

  • Without roads, markets, and human capital, former mita residents are far more likely to be subsistence farmers today
  • Selling to markets rewards investment and specialization; subsistence farming locks in low productivity
  • The disparity in public goods reshaped the entire structure of the local economy

The persistence chain

  1. Mita → no secure property rights, few large landowners
  2. Few landowners → nobody powerful lobbying → fewer public goods (roads, schools)
  3. Fewer public goods → weak market access and human capital → subsistence farming and lower consumption today
  • An institution can outlive itself by changing what gets built — physically and legally

Nation building: Vietnam

Can outsiders build institutions?

  • If institutions matter this much, can foreign intervention create stable ones?
  • The U.S. has tried repeatedly — regime change, public-opinion campaigns — especially amid conflict
  • Costly interventions, thin evidence: you cannot randomize military strategy
  • But big data + historical analysis can still open a window

Dell and Querubin (2018): two strategies in Vietnam

  • Goal: strengthen the South Vietnamese government, weaken the Viet Cong
  • Overwhelming firepower (Air Force): establish control through air strikes — make opposing the state costly
  • Hearts and minds (Marines): provide public goods, economic opportunity, civic engagement
  • Which approach worked?

An RD hiding in a rounding rule

  • The Air Force scored hamlets on insurgency risk: 169 monthly and quarterly questions → a statistical model → a continuous risk score from 1 to 5
  • Scores were rounded to the nearest integer to decide bombing
  • So a hamlet scoring 4.50 was far more likely to be bombed than a nearly identical hamlet scoring 4.49
  • Dell and Querubin recovered the scoring algorithm from unclassified documents at Fort McNair and tapes at the National Archives

What did bombing do?

  • Bombing increased armed Viet Cong presence and VC recruitment
  • Weakened local governance: fewer village committee positions filled, less access to primary school, less civic participation
  • Firepower moved every institution-building outcome the wrong way

Hearts and minds: another spatial RD

  • A sharp border separated where the Marines (hearts and minds) vs. the Army (conventional force) operated
  • Same logic as the mita boundary: compare hamlets just inside vs. just outside the Marines’ zone

Lessons for nation building

  • Military force may worsen security and undermine local government relative to a hearts-and-minds approach
  • Citizens have many ways to quietly undermine a state they don’t support
  • Broader point: careful empirical evaluation is feasible even for questions as hard as military strategy

Wrapping up

Recap

  • Income gaps across countries are enormous — up to tenfold
  • Institutions hypothesis (North): property rights and governance drive development
  • Dell (2010): the mita boundary is a spatial RD — consumption ~25% lower and stunting ~30% higher inside, 200 years after abolition
  • Persistence channels: property rights → public goods → markets
  • Vietnam: bombing backfired; hearts and minds built institutions

The method, one more time

  • Spatial regression discontinuity: treatment flips at a line on the map; compare places just on either side
  • Plus historical data analysis: archives from 1689 linked to modern surveys
  • Same RD logic you used in Project 2 — cutoffs in scores, lines on maps, thresholds in rules are everywhere
