Differences in economic opportunity

Today’s question

Why have Black–white income gaps persisted for generations — and what would it actually take to close them?

  • New lens for this Part: differences in opportunity across groups, not just places
  • Core source: Chetty, Hendren, Jones, and Porter (2020) — your reading for today
  • New method: dynamic models and steady states

  • The gaps today, and the classic theories for them
  • A thought experiment: what if mobility were race-invariant?
  • What the data show instead: race-specific mobility and steady states
  • The gap is a story about boys, not girls

  • These gaps have persisted for generations — the question is why

Three families of theories

  • Family-level factors — parental income and wealth, parental education, family structure
  • Structural features of the environment — segregation and neighborhoods (Wilson 1987), school quality, discrimination in labor markets and criminal justice
  • Cultural factors and social norms — identity, aspirations, role models
  • Decades of work in each row — but mostly comparing people within a single generation

Today’s lens: an intergenerational perspective

  • Most prior work: snapshot disparities in one generation
  • We instead follow incomes across generations — the dynamics
  • Why it pays off:
    • Tells us which disparities will persist in the long run
    • Isolates the factors that drive persistent gaps
  • Method for today: dynamics of income and steady states (Chetty et al. 2020)

A thought experiment: race-invariant mobility

The tool we already have: the rank–rank line

  • Recall from Class 2: mean child income rank vs. parent income rank
  • Nationally the line is nearly straight with slope 0.35 (Chetty et al. 2014)
  • Kids of the richest parents end up ~35 percentiles above kids of the poorest parents, on average
  • Suppose — hypothetically — Black and white children faced this same line. What happens to the gap?

TopHat: How big is the gap in generation 2?

The gap shrinks by the slope (0.35) each generation: 25.2 → 8.8 → ?

  • A: about 6 percentiles
  • B: about 3 percentiles
  • C: about 9 percentiles
  • D: 0 — it disappears entirely

Generation 2: the gap falls to 3.1

Exponential convergence

  • Each generation: new gap = slope × old gap = 0.35 × 25.2 ≈ 8.8
  • Generation 2: 0.35 × 8.8 ≈ 3.1 percentiles (52.1 vs. 49.0)
  • If mobility did not vary by race, racial disparities would shrink rapidly — at an exponential rate across generations (Becker and Tomes 1979)
  • Centuries of persistent gaps are therefore a puzzle… unless mobility itself differs by race

What the data show

Mobility is not race-invariant

  • Plot the rank–rank line separately by race — the lines do not coincide (Chetty et al. 2020)
  • Conditional on parent income, Black children land lower than white children at every parent rank:
    • at p25: 12.6 percentiles lower
    • at p75: 15.7 percentiles lower
    • at p100: 12.4 percentiles lower

The lines do not coincide

Source: Chetty, Hendren, Jones, and Porter (2020)

Lower up, faster down

  • Black children have lower upward mobility from the bottom and higher downward mobility from the top
  • Black children raised in the highest-income families still end up with substantially lower adult incomes than white children from similarly affluent families
  • A Black child from a top-quintile family has a high chance of falling to the bottom fifth; a white child from a top-quintile family tends to stay near the top

Method spotlight: dynamic models and steady states

  • A dynamic model tracks a variable generation by generation: next generation’s rank = f(this generation’s rank)
  • A steady state is the resting point: where the next generation equals the current one — the system stops moving
  • On our chart: where the mobility line crosses the 45-degree line
  • If the slope is below 1, the system converges to that point from any starting rank

Finding each group’s steady state

  • Whites’ mobility line crosses the 45-degree line at rank 54.4
  • Blacks’ mobility line crosses it at rank 35.2
  • Steady-state gap: 19.2 percentiles
  • Compare: the gap we observe today is about 21 percentiles

The gap we see today is (almost) the long-run gap

  • Today’s gap ≈ steady-state gap → Black Americans are close to their steady state
  • So the Black–white gap is driven by intergenerational gaps in mobility, not transitory factors that will fade on their own
  • Waiting will not close this gap
  • To change the long run, you must change rates of mobility — one-time income transfers shift one generation, then the dynamics pull incomes right back

Other groups tell a consistent story

  • Native Americans: mobility line nearly identical to Black Americans’ — low incomes expected to persist
  • Hispanic Americans: line much closer to whites’ — currently low incomes, but predicted to converge upward across generations
  • Asian Americans: line looks almost flat — parent income barely predicts child income

Boys vs. girls

Male children: the familiar gap

Female children: the gap vanishes

The gap among boys — and among girls

  • Male children: familiar picture — Black boys land about 9.7 percentiles lower at p25 and 12.0 lower at p75
  • Female children: the gap essentially vanishes — differences of −1.4 at p25 and −1.0 at p75
  • Conditional on parent income, Black women earn slightly more than white women
  • The Black–white intergenerational gap is overwhelmingly a story about men (Chetty et al. 2020)

Careful: “conditional on parent income” ≠ “on average”

  • On average, Black women still earn much less than white women — because they grow up in far lower-income families
  • And women’s household incomes in the next generation depend on marriage — so men’s lower mobility still pulls down Black households’ steady state
  • No gender gap in mobility does not mean no racial gap for women in the long run

Employment tells the same story

  • Only about 55% of Black men raised in the lowest-income families are employed in a given year
  • At p25, the Black–white employment gap among men is about 19 percentage points
  • Black and white women: nearly identical employment rates conditional on parent income (Black women slightly higher)

Incarceration

  • 21% of Black men raised in the lowest-income families were incarcerated on the day of the 2010 Census (ages 27–32)
  • That is one day’s snapshot — not a lifetime rate
  • White men’s incarceration also rises at the bottom, but nowhere near that level; women’s rates are far lower for both groups
  • Even Black men with no criminal-justice contact are on very different trajectories than white men — incarceration is part of the story, not all of it

What does not explain the gap

Family-level factors don’t account for it

  • Natural suspects: parental wealth, parental education, two-parent vs. single-parent households
  • Test: compare Black and white men raised in two-parent families with comparable income, education, and wealth
  • Result: Black men still fare substantially worse (Chetty et al. 2020)
  • So the drivers must include environmental factors beyond the family → study how gaps vary across places

Reading the two maps together

  • Same color scale on both maps — and they barely overlap
  • The best commuting zones for Black men have upward mobility comparable to the worst ones for white men
  • Examples: raised poor in Boston, Black men average $24k, white men $31k; San Francisco $19k vs. $31k
  • There is essentially no place in America without a substantial Black–white gap in upward mobility

Zoom in: from regions to neighborhoods

  • Commuting zones show broad regions — but Black and white families live in different neighborhoods within them
  • So zoom to Census tracts (~4,200 people)
  • Chetty et al. (2020) summarize the tract-level evidence in four key results

Result 1: the gap is everywhere

  • Controlling for parental income, Black boys have lower earnings than white boys in 99% of Census tracts in America
  • Even when Black and white boys grow up in the same neighborhood, attend the same schools, with parents earning similar amounts — the gap remains

Result 2: “good” neighborhoods help — but widen the gap

  • Both Black and white boys do better in low-poverty, higher-rent tracts with better schools
  • But nearly every marker of a “good” neighborhood — low poverty, test scores, college graduates, two-parent share — predicts more improvement for white boys than Black boys
  • So the Black–white gap is larger in affluent neighborhoods than in disadvantaged ones

What predicts good outcomes — for whom

The gap widens as tract poverty falls

Result 3: what predicts better Black outcomes

  • Within low-poverty areas, two factors predict better outcomes for Black boys and smaller gaps:
    • Father presence: Black men raised in tracts where more Black fathers are present have much higher employment rates
    • Less racial bias: areas scoring lower on a Google-search-based index of racial animus have better outcomes for Black men
  • Descriptive, not proven causes — but consistent with discrimination and role-model channels

Father presence and the employment gap

These exposure effects are race- and gender-specific

  • Black father presence in a tract predicts outcomes for Black men — but is essentially unrelated to outcomes for white men in the same tract
  • It is also unrelated to outcomes for Black women
  • So this is not just a proxy for “better schools” or other broad amenities — the relevant environment differs by race and gender

Within Black families: men respond, women don’t

Result 4: these are causal exposure effects

  • Reuse the movers design from Part 1: children who move to a better area at a younger age gain more
  • Black boys who move earlier to places where Black men do well have better outcomes — in proportion to years of exposure
  • Moving to a place where white men do well does not deliver the same gains
  • The Black–white gap itself is causally shaped by childhood exposure to neighborhoods

The catch: those neighborhoods barely exist

  • Define a “good” tract: poverty rate < 10% and father presence above 50% among children of the child’s own race
  • 62.5% of white children grow up in such tracts
  • 4.2% of Black children do — while 66% of Black children grow up in high-poverty, low-father-presence tracts
  • Black boys thrive where resources are good and race-specific factors are favorable — but almost no American neighborhood offers both

Almost no neighborhoods offer both

What it means for policy

Why many proposed policies fall short

  • Cash transfers, tax credits, minimum wages: raise incomes now, but the dynamics pull the next generation back — no persistent effect unless mobility rates change
  • Reducing residential or school segregation: can raise the level of outcomes for both groups — but the gap persists in 99% of tracts, so integration alone may not narrow it

What might actually narrow the gap

  • Policies that cut within neighborhoods and improve environments for specific subgroups — especially Black boys and men:
    • Mentoring programs for Black boys (Heller et al. 2015)
    • Efforts to reduce racial bias (Devine et al. 2012)
    • Actual integration within schools — contact, not just co-enrollment
    • Criminal justice reform
  • Developing and evaluating such programs is a major open frontier

Recap

  • If mobility were race-invariant, a 25-percentile gap would shrink to ~3 in two generations — persistence means mobility differs by race
  • Steady-state gap (19.2) ≈ today’s gap (~21): the disparity is self-reproducing, not transitory
  • The intergenerational gap is a story about boys: employment and incarceration gaps are enormous; girls’ gap is ~0 conditional on parent income
  • Family-level factors don’t explain it; neighborhoods matter, but the gap survives in 99% of tracts and widens in affluent ones
  • Only 4.2% of Black children grow up in low-poverty, high-father-presence tracts

Exposure to the U.S. justice system

Today’s question

When a parent is arrested, charged, or locked up, a child’s life changes too. How many children does the U.S. justice system reach — and could better predictions make its decisions better?

  • First half: counting children’s exposure (Finlay, Mueller-Smith, and Street 2023 — today’s reading)
  • Second half: machine learning as a tool for improving justice decisions
  • We end with a hard question: what does “fair” mean for an algorithm?

Where we’re headed

  • Method spotlight: machine learning, taught through bail hearings (Kleinberg et al. 2018)

From income gaps to the justice system

Where last class pointed

Source: Chetty, Hendren, Jones, and Porter (2020).

What that figure showed

  • The Black–white income gap is concentrated among men (Chetty et al. 2020)
  • One outcome stood out: incarceration measured on a single day (April 1, 2010)
  • Among men raised in families at the 25th percentile, the Black incarceration rate was about 8 percentage points higher than the white rate
  • Among Black men from the very poorest families: roughly 1 in 5 incarcerated that day

Today we flip the lens to children

  • Justice-system contact is usually measured for defendants
  • But arrests, charges, and incarceration reach into households: lost income, instability, stigma
  • Question 1: how many children are touched, and which children?
  • Question 2: can the decisions that produce this exposure be improved?

Counting children’s exposure

Why counting is surprisingly hard

  • Surveys: small samples; usually ask only about parental incarceration, not arrests or charges; stigma leads to underreporting
  • Administrative records: near-complete for defendants, but say nothing about the defendant’s children
  • No single dataset contains both the justice event and the family
  • Sound familiar? The Opportunity Atlas faced the same problem for income

The data: CJARS linked to the Census

  • CJARS — the Criminal Justice Administrative Records System (Finlay and Mueller-Smith 2020): millions of arrest, charge, conviction, and incarceration records, harmonized across agencies nationwide (Univ. of Michigan + Census Bureau)
  • Linked, anonymized, to billions of federal tax, household survey (2000/2010 Census and ACS), and program participation records
  • The household rosters reveal which children live with each adult in the criminal records — exposure through biological parents or co-resident adults
  • Same recipe as the Atlas — link survey and administrative big data — applied to a new domain (Finlay et al. 2023)

TopHat: Guess before the reveal

By their 18th birthday, what share of American children have had a parent charged with a crime?

  • A: about 5%
  • B: about 15%
  • C: about 25%
  • D: about 40%

Exposure is far more common than we thought

For children born 1999–2005, by age 18:

  • Parent charged with any crime: ~39%
  • Parent with a felony conviction: ~18%
  • Parent in prison: ~9%

Roughly two in five children — parental justice contact is a mainstream feature of American childhood, not a rare event.

Exposure by race and by income

Source: Finlay, Mueller-Smith, and Street (2023).

Exposure differs sharply by race

  • Charge exposure exceeds 60% for Black and Native American children — nearly double the white rate (32%)
  • Parental prison exposure: 20% of Black and 15% of Native American children, vs. 6% of white children — roughly triple
  • The ranking of exposure (any charge > felony > incarceration) is the same for every group — the levels differ dramatically
  • Because white children are the largest group, they are still the largest number of exposed children

Exposure differs sharply by income

  • Steep income gradient: the lower the household’s income, the higher the child’s exposure to every justice event
  • Among children from the lowest-income households, any-charge exposure also exceeds 60% (panel B)
  • Race gaps remain within income groups — Black children at a given household income are still more exposed than white children at the same income
  • Echo of last class: family income alone does not explain racial gaps

Exposure varies across places too

Source: Finlay, Mueller-Smith, and Street (2023).

Reading the county map

  • Exposure varies enormously across counties, even within the same state
  • Grey areas: states not yet in the CJARS sample, or counties too small to measure — even “big” administrative data takes years to assemble
  • Place, race, and income all shape which childhoods the justice system touches

Why this matters for opportunity

  • Children with justice-exposed parents fare worse on schooling, adult income, and their own justice contact
  • Careful: these are correlations — the disadvantages that predict a parent’s arrest also predict child outcomes directly
  • But the sheer scale means the justice system is a first-order part of the environment children grow up in
  • One policy handle: make the system’s decisions better — our second half

The bail decision

Thousands of small, fast decisions

  • After an arrest, a judge quickly decides: hold the defendant in jail until trial, or release them
  • Legal objective: minimize the risk of failure to appear at trial
  • Stakes run both ways: jail can cost a job, housing, custody of kids; wrongful release risks missed trials and new crime
  • At its core this is a prediction problem — decided by a human in minutes

Are human decisions biased? Evidence 1: resumes

  • Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004): 5,000 fictitious resumes sent to 1,300 real job ads in Boston and Chicago
  • Identical credentials; only the name randomized — white-sounding vs. Black-sounding
  • White-sounding names received about 50% more callbacks
  • A randomized audit study: discrimination conditional on credentials

Evidence 2: Airbnb hosts

  • Edelman et al.: fictitious guest requests to real listings; names randomized, no profile photos
  • Hosts accepted guests with white-sounding names significantly more often
  • About 40% of the rejecting listings sat vacant on the requested dates
  • Turning away Black guests cost hosts roughly $65–100 each — bias persists even when it is expensive

Evidence 3: decision fatigue

  • Danziger et al.: Israeli parole hearings, about 20 cases per judge per day, heard in near-random order
  • Right after a meal break: parole granted about 60% of the time
  • Just before the next break: close to zero
  • (Later work debates how much reflects case scheduling — but the broader point stands: human cognition is limited, hungry, and noisy)

Method spotlight: machine learning

What machine learning is

  • Algorithms that learn a prediction rule from data instead of being hand-programmed
  • Input: many predictors (age, current charge, prior record, …)
  • Output: a predicted probability of an outcome for each new case
  • ML answers “who is risky?” — a prediction question
  • It does not answer “what would jailing this person cause?” — a causal question (more on this next class)

Kleinberg et al. (2018): the setup

  • About 750,000 bail cases from New York City, 2008–2013
  • The algorithm sees only what the judge sees at the hearing — the rap sheet, the current charge
  • Outcome data: whether released defendants failed to appear at trial
  • Goal: compare the machine’s risk ranking to judges’ actual jail-or-release choices

Step 1: split your data

  • Lock box: a slice set aside, untouched, to check the final model
  • Training set (~80% of the rest): the data used to fit the model
  • Hold-out set (~20%): used to evaluate the model — data it has never seen
  • Never grade a model on the data used to fit it: that test is circular and the model will always look good

The enemy: overfitting

  • Give a model enough variables and it can fit its own training data almost perfectly
  • But then it is fitting the noise, not the signal
  • Overfit models look brilliant in-sample and fall apart on new defendants
  • The only score that counts is out-of-sample performance

TopHat: overfitting check

A model predicts its training data with 99% accuracy. What do we know about how it will predict next year’s cases?

  • A: It will be about 99% accurate
  • B: It will do worse — possibly far worse
  • C: It will do better — more data by then
  • D: Nothing, until we test it on data it has never seen

The workhorse: decision trees

  • Ask: which single variable, if we split the data on it, best separates those who appear at trial from those who don’t? Split there first (say, felony vs. misdemeanor charge)
  • Within each branch, repeat: split again on the next most predictive variable
  • Each final “leaf” gives a predicted risk for defendants who match that path
  • Simple and interpretable — the building block of modern ML methods

How deep should the tree grow?

  • Deeper tree = more flexibility = more risk of overfitting
  • 5-fold cross-validation: split training data into 5 folds; fit on 4, measure error on the 5th; rotate
  • Out-of-sample error falls, bottoms out (near depth 35 here), then rises — that turn is overfitting appearing
  • In-sample error falls forever — which is exactly why it is the wrong guide

Machine vs. judge

The machine’s decision rule

  • Every defendant gets a predicted probability of failing to appear
  • The rule is a simple threshold: jail above it, release below it
  • Slide the threshold to match any jail rate you like — so comparisons with judges are apples to apples
  • All evaluation happens on the hold-out sample

What judges actually do

  • Predicted risk tracks observed failure-to-appear closely — the model works out of sample
  • Yet judges release about half of defendants whose predicted risk exceeds 60%
  • And judges jail about 30% of the lowest-risk defendants (predicted risk under 20%)
  • Misranking in both directions

The headline result

  • Re-sort: jail the predicted-riskiest, release the predicted-safest
  • Crime (failures to appear) falls about 25% with no change in how many people are jailed
  • Or: the jail population falls about 42% with no increase in crime
  • Fewer crimes, or thousands fewer people behind bars — from the same information judges already had

Why does the machine win?

  • Decision fatigue and time pressure — the parole study in miniature, thousands of times a day
  • Judges weight vivid but weakly predictive cues — how someone carries themselves in the courtroom
  • The model pools information from hundreds of thousands of cases and never has a bad afternoon
  • Judges are not bad people; they are people

Replace judges? No — inform them

  • Almost no one argues for fully automated bail
  • The realistic design: show the judge a risk score; the judge may deviate from it
  • Keeps human judgment for what the data miss; disciplines the part the data measure well
  • Several jurisdictions already use tools like this

Predictive policing

Crime clusters in place and time

  • Look at burglaries within 0.1 miles of each other: the distribution of time gaps between them spikes at 0–1 days
  • A burglary today strongly predicts another burglary on the same block tomorrow
  • Prediction opportunity: direct patrols toward emerging hot spots rather than yesterday’s map

Listening for crime: ShotSpotter

  • Rooftop microphones detect the audio signature of gunfire — a machine learning classifier for sound
  • Useful because only a small fraction of gunshots is ever reported to police
  • Gives high-frequency data on where gun crime is actually occurring
  • Individual-level prediction (school records, social networks) is also possible — and much more ethically fraught

The fairness debate

TopHat: Where do you land?

Should bail judges be shown an algorithm’s risk score for each defendant?

  • A: Yes — it prevents crimes and needless jailings
  • B: Only with regular audits for racial disparities
  • C: No — people shouldn’t be judged by other people’s behavior
  • D: Genuinely unsure

Concern: judged by your group, not your acts

  • Prediction treats you according to the behavior of people who share your attributes
  • Dropping race from the model does not fix this: correlated proxies — like ZIP code — pull it right back in
  • Result: more policing or jail for things you haven’t done
  • A serious ethical cost, not a technicality

The other side: discarding information has costs too

  • The status quo is not neutral: we just saw biased, noisy human decisions
  • Refusing good predictions means more crime and more people needlessly jailed — burdens that also fall unequally
  • The real comparison is not “algorithm vs. perfect justice” but “algorithm vs. judges as they actually are”
  • Reasonable people weigh process fairness and outcomes differently

An open research frontier

  • A growing “algorithmic fairness” literature makes fairness criteria precise and quantifies the tradeoffs
  • Uncomfortable result: reasonable definitions of fairness can conflict — a society must choose among them
  • The economist’s job: make the tradeoffs explicit instead of leaving them buried inside a black box

Recap

  • Linked CJARS–Census data: by age 18, 39% of children have had a parent charged with a crime, 18% a parent with a felony conviction, 9% a parent in prison — with stark race and income gradients (Finlay et al. 2023)
  • Bail is a prediction problem, and human predictions are biased and noisy (resume and Airbnb audits, decision fatigue)
  • ML discipline: training vs. hold-out data and cross-validation guard against overfitting
  • Kleinberg et al. (2018): crime −25% at the same jail rate, or jailing −42% at the same crime rate
  • The fairness debate has real arguments on both sides

Detecting an outbreak

Public health in the era of big data

  • Public health and epidemiology try to improve health by:
    • Changing individual behavior — smoking, exercise, vaccination
    • Improving population health — water quality, slowing contagion
  • Big data is entering the field through one classic question: can we forecast an epidemic before it arrives?

Why speed is everything

  • Contagious diseases spread exponentially, not linearly
  • One week of delay does not cost you one week of cases — it can double them
  • Huge returns to acting early: closing schools, shipping vaccine, staffing hospitals
  • So the value of surveillance data is mostly about timeliness

The traditional system

  • CDC’s outpatient surveillance network (ILINet): a few thousand volunteer clinics report the share of visits for influenza-like illness
  • Strengths: it is a real measurement of real patients — ground truth
  • Weaknesses:
    • Reported with a one- to two-week lag
    • Small samples → only nine broad regions, not neighborhoods

Surveillance in action: EVALI, 2019

  • Autumn 2019: clinicians started admitting young, healthy patients with severe lung injury
  • Ohio hospitals were among them; the Columbus Dispatch ran the story in November 2019
  • CDC named it EVALI — e-cigarette or vaping product use–associated lung injury
  • By mid-October 2019: 1,479 confirmed cases and 33 deaths

What surveillance found

  • 82% of hospitalized EVALI patients reported using THC-containing products
  • Vitamin E acetate, an additive in illicit THC cartridges, was the common thread
  • Cases peaked the week of September 15, 2019, then fell steadily
  • Note the pattern: the clinical reporting system was slow, but it was the thing that identified the actual cause

EVALI case rates and e-cigarette use, by state marijuana policy

What the EVALI chart suggests

  • States with legal recreational markets had lower case rates — even though vaping rates were the same
  • Suggestive, not causal: states differ in many ways at once

Google Flu Trends

A very good idea

  • Ginsberg et al. (2009), published in Nature, propose a new data source: Google search queries
  • Logic: people search “how to treat a cough” or “antibiotics” before they ever see a doctor
  • Search data are:
    • Immediate — no reporting lag
    • Fine-grained — available by state, even city
  • Google knew who was searching what, in real time, everywhere

What is being predicted, and with what

  • Outcome (the truth): CDC influenza-like illness rates
    • Weekly, 9 U.S. regions, 2004–2007
    • 128 weeks × 9 regions = 1,152 observations
  • Predictors: weekly Google search counts for 50 million distinct search terms
  • Notice the shape of that dataset

“Wide” data

  • Long data: many rows, few columns (the Opportunity Atlas — 20.5 million kids, a handful of variables)
  • Wide data: more variables than observations
  • Here: 50,000,000 candidate predictors, 1,152 observations
  • Ordinary regression simply breaks — you can fit 1,152 data points perfectly with 1,152 variables

Overfitting

  • Overfitting: a model so flexible it fits the noise in your sample, not the signal in the world
  • A perfectly fit model has zero error in your data and tells you nothing about the next observation
  • The tell: fit is spectacular in-sample, terrible out-of-sample
  • With 50 million predictors, overfitting is not a risk — it is the default

TopHat: Two models of the same flu data

Model A explains 99% of the variation in the data used to build it. Model B explains 75%. Which would you deploy?

  • A — it fits better
  • B — 99% smells like overfitting
  • Not enough information

Method spotlight: out-of-sample validation

The fix: never grade your own homework

  • Core idea: use different data to build the model than to judge it
  • If you estimate and evaluate on the same rows, the exercise is circular — of course it fits, you chose it to fit
  • Standard practice splits the data into pieces with different jobs

Three jobs for your data

Piece Job
Training set Estimate the model (typically ~80%)
Validation set Tune choices: how many predictors, how complex
Hold-out / “lock box” Touched once, at the end, to report honest performance
  • The lock box is set aside before you look at anything
  • Kleinberg et al.’s bail study used exactly this structure

Cross-validation

  • Problem: splitting the data wastes data
  • k-fold cross-validation: split the training data into k pieces (say 5)
    • Estimate on 4 folds, evaluate on the 5th
    • Rotate, so every fold gets a turn as the evaluator
    • Average the 5 prediction errors
  • Every row helps train and helps evaluate — just never at the same time

The two error curves

  • In-sample error falls forever: add a variable, fit the same rows better. Always.
  • Out-of-sample error is U-shaped: it falls, hits a minimum, then rises
  • The minimum is the model you want. Everything to the right is overfitting.
  • In-sample fit is the wrong notion of prediction error — full stop

GFT step 1: rank 50 million terms

  • Run one regression per search term — 50 million separate regressions of CDC flu rates on that term’s search volume
  • Compute how well each term’s predictions correlate with CDC data across the nine regions
  • Keep the top 100 terms
  • The list includes “cough” and “antibiotics” — and also “high school basketball” and “oscar nominations”

GFT step 2: how many terms?

  • Add up search volume across the top n terms, varying n from 1 to 100
  • Judge each choice of n on a separate set of later weeks — data not used to rank the terms
  • Best fit: the sum of the top 45 queries
  • This is out-of-sample validation choosing a tuning parameter

Out-of-sample fit peaks at 45 queries

Source: Ginsberg et al. (2009)

Why “oscar nominations” is a warning light

  • Nothing about the Oscars causes influenza
  • It correlates because Oscar season and flu season are both winter
  • A term like that predicts fine — until the calendar relationship drifts
  • Black-box fit is easy; a model with a sensible mechanism is more likely to keep working

GFT step 3: the real test

  • Final step: forecast the 2007–08 flu season — data that did not exist when the model was built
  • Predictions tracked CDC ILI rates closely, with correlations around 0.97 across the nine regions
  • And it ran one to two weeks ahead of CDC’s official reports
  • Genuinely impressive. It made the cover of Nature.

The collapse

Then it broke

Google Flu Trends vs. CDC influenza-like-illness rates, 2009–2013. Source: Lazer et al. (2014)

What the picture shows

  • Starting in 2011, Google Flu Trends overshot CDC in 100 of 108 weeks
  • February 2013: Google’s estimate was more than double the actual CDC rate
  • Lazer et al. (2014) diagnosed the failure simply — by extending the out-of-sample window to 2013
  • Note the pale blue line: lagged CDC data alone did about as well

Why did it break?

  • Google changed the search engine, not the flu
  • Autocomplete began suggesting search terms as you typed
  • After a search for “fever” or “cough,” Google started recommending related diagnoses to click
  • More flu searches per sick person → the model read the same epidemic as much bigger
  • The model was never re-estimated, so the drift compounded

The deeper failures

  • No ground truth check. Search data measure searching, not sickness.
  • The data-generating process was not stable. Google’s own product team kept changing it.
  • Big data hubris: treating volume as a substitute for measurement and theory (Lazer et al. 2014)
  • The obvious benchmark — what CDC reported three weeks ago — was never taken seriously

TopHat: What was the single best fix?

Google Flu Trends is overshooting badly. You get one change. Which?

  • Add more search terms
  • Re-estimate the model on recent data every season
  • Combine the search index with lagged CDC data
  • Shut it down and wait for CDC

Lessons from the parable

  • Big data has real potential for prediction — this idea was good
  • But big data is not a substitute for ground truth
  • Good thing CDC never shut down its clinic reporting network
  • Models need judgment, not just fit: prefer mechanisms you can defend
  • Google Flu Trends stopped publishing estimates in 2015

Prediction vs. causation

Two different questions

Question Tool Example
What will happen? Prediction Which defendants skip trial?
What happens if I act? Causation Does jail reduce future crime?
  • Everything in Part 1 and Part 2 was causal: MTO, the class-size discontinuity, teacher value-added
  • Today’s material is the other column

Umbrellas and rain dances

  • Kleinberg et al. (2015) put it this way:
    • Umbrella problem: should I carry one? I only need to predict rain. The umbrella’s effect is known.
    • Rain dance problem: will dancing bring rain? Only a causal effect answers it.
  • Many important policy problems are umbrella problems in disguise

When prediction is genuinely enough

  • Which neighborhoods will see a burglary tonight → where to patrol
  • Which patients are so sick that surgery will not extend life → which operations are futile
  • Which regions are about to see a flu surge → where to ship vaccine
  • In each case the action’s effect is known; the open question is who and where

When prediction is not enough

  • “Kids from this ZIP code have low incomes” → do not conclude that moving them raises income
  • That is a causal claim, and it needed the Moving to Opportunity experiment to settle
  • A model can predict outcomes perfectly and still be useless for policy
  • Ask yourself: am I choosing whom to act on, or whether the action works?

Predictions change when you use them

  • The flu model broke because the world it measured changed
  • Policy makes this worse: acting on a prediction changes the behavior you were predicting
  • Predictive policing sends officers somewhere → arrests rise there → the data say that place is dangerous
  • A measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure
