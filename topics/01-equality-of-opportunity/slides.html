TopHat: Rags to riches

A child is born to parents in the bottom fifth of the U.S. income distribution. What is the chance that child reaches the top fifth as an adult?

  • A. About 25%
  • B. About 15%
  • C. About 7.5%
  • D. About 1%

The answer: 7.5%

  • In the U.S., a bottom-fifth child has a 7.5% chance of reaching the top fifth (Chetty et al. 2014)
  • Higher elsewhere: roughly 9% in the U.K., 11.7% in Denmark, 13.5% in Canada
  • “Land of opportunity” is a testable claim — and the U.S. lags its peers
  • But national averages hide enormous variation within the U.S. — more on that soon

Why is the dream fading?

  • Central policy question: why are children’s chances of climbing the income ladder falling — and how do we reverse it?
  • Hard to answer from historical macro trends alone
  • Too many things changed at once; explanations are hard to tell apart
  • Core problem: only a handful of data points

From theory to data

Social science used to be theory-first

  • Until recently, social scientists had limited data on questions like these
  • Economics built mathematical models; sociology built qualitative theories
  • Theories were used to explain patterns and recommend policy
  • The problem: with five economists you often get five different answers
  • Untested theories get politicized — e.g., “is Obamacare reducing job growth?” became a political fight, not a scientific one

Case study: does health reform cost jobs?

  • Much discussion, little (but growing) evidence
  • Pundits and headlines answered before the data did — reform was both “killing jobs” and “adding jobs,” depending on the outlet
  • One way to make progress: find a place that tried the same policy earlier
  • Massachusetts passed a nearly identical reform in 2006 — the model for the national law (“we used the same advisers, and they say it’s the same plan” — President Obama, first presidential debate, 2012)

Big data accelerates the trend

  • Government data: tax records, Medicare

  • Unstructured data: Twitter, newspapers
  • Datasets that transformed business are now transforming social science

Why big data changes what we can learn

  • Greater reliability than surveys
  • New variables we could never measure before (e.g., emotions from text)
  • Universal coverage — we can zoom in on small subgroups and places
  • Samples large enough to approximate scientific experiments

Two types of big data: “long” data

Many observations, few variables — e.g., tax records

Two types of big data: “wide” data

Few observations, many variables — e.g., Amazon clicks, newspaper text

Two cultures, two goals

  • Statistics and computer science focus on wide data → prediction
    • Example: predict income to target ads
  • Social science focuses on long data → causal effects
    • Example: does improving schools raise income?
  • This course teaches both — and when each is the right tool

  • B. $10,000

Computing any percentile

  1. Sort the data from smallest to largest
  2. Row i of N covers the (i-1)/N to i/N percentile range
  3. Find the row whose range contains the percentile you want
  • With 7 incomes, the 4th row spans the 43rd–57th percentile → the median is $10,000
  • Note the median ignores how extreme the extremes are — unlike the mean

Measuring incomes as ranks

  • Parents: average household income on Form 1040, 1994–2000
  • Children: income from tax returns in 2014–15, at ages 31–37
  • Everything converted to percentile ranks in the national distribution
  • Children ranked against children born the same year; parents against other parents

Aside: what is correlation?

  • A measure of the relationship between two variables
  • At its simplest: how strongly two variables move together linearly
  • Always ranges from −1 to 1
  • 0 means no linear relationship; the sign gives the direction

Rank–rank: one number per place

Children’s rank vs. parents’ rank in Chicago

Reading the rank–rank plot

  • x-axis: parent rank in the national income distribution
  • y-axis: average child rank in adulthood, one dot per parent percentile
  • Strong upward slope: richer parents, richer kids — and strikingly linear (Chetty et al. 2014)

One summary statistic per place

  • Fit a regression line through the rank–rank dots
  • Read off the predicted child rank when parents are at the 25th percentile (parents earning about $27,000)
  • Chicago: children from 25th-percentile families reach the 40th percentile on average — about $30,400 in adult income
  • We call this absolute upward mobility at p25

Why fit a line instead of using the raw dot?

  • Small areas have few observations — individual dots are noisy
  • A regression line pools information across the whole distribution
  • That is the core idea of a statistical model: represent the data in a more stable way
  • Linearity in ranks is what makes this trick work so well

From Chicago to every neighborhood in America

  • Run this regression separately for each Census tract — about 70,000 tracts of roughly 4,200 people each
  • Many children move during childhood
  • Solution: weight each child by the fraction of childhood (to age
    1. spent in each tract
  • Result: an upward-mobility estimate for every neighborhood in the country

What high-mobility places have in common

Does job growth mean opportunity?

Source: Chetty et al. 2018, Opportunity Atlas

Job growth is not the same as opportunity

  • First guess: booming labor markets lift kids up
  • The data say no: across the 30 largest metros, job growth and upward mobility are essentially unrelated
  • Charlotte: among the fastest job growth in America, near the bottom in mobility
  • A hot labor market for adults today is not the same as opportunity for kids growing up there

Five strongest correlates of upward mobility

  1. Segregation — more racial and income segregation, less mobility
  2. Income inequality — a smaller middle class, less mobility
  3. School quality — higher spending, smaller classes, higher test scores, more mobility
  4. Family structure — more single-parent households, less mobility
  5. Social capital — more community connectedness, more mobility

Sorting or causation?

Two explanations for place differences

Children’s outcomes vary enormously across neighborhoods. Why?

  • Sorting: different people live in different places
    • Families in high-mobility areas may differ in education, wealth, health
  • Causal effects: places themselves change outcomes
    • Move the same child from a red area to a blue area and their adult income changes
  • The map alone cannot tell these apart — correlation is not causation

Why the answer matters for policy

  • If it’s sorting → invest in people, wherever they live
  • If it’s causal effects → invest in places, or help families reach better ones
  • Billions of dollars in housing policy ride on this distinction

The ideal experiment

  • Randomly assign children to neighborhoods at birth; compare adult outcomes
  • Obviously we can’t do that literally
  • But in 1994, the U.S. government ran something surprisingly close
  • First, we need the logic of experiments

The logic of randomized experiments

The question, stated precisely

Will a child’s adult earnings be higher if their family gets a housing voucher?

  • Y_i(V=1) = child i’s earnings if the family gets a voucher
  • Y_i(V=0) = child i’s earnings if the family does not
  • Treatment effect for child i: G_i = Y_i(V=1) - Y_i(V=0)

The fundamental problem

  • We never observe both potential outcomes for the same child
  • Each family either got a voucher or didn’t — we see only one branch
  • Every causal question in empirical science hits this wall
  • Solution: estimate the average effect across comparable groups

Randomized experiments: the gold standard

  • Take 10,000 children; flip a coin for each family: voucher or no voucher
  • Compare average earnings in the two groups
  • Randomization makes the groups identical except for the voucher
  • So the difference in averages is the average treatment effect
  • Tech firms call the same design “A/B testing”

Why not just compare applicants?

  • Suppose we compared families who applied for vouchers to families who didn’t
  • Any earnings gap could reflect other differences:
    • Applicants may be more educated, more motivated
    • Or may live in worse neighborhoods to begin with
  • Randomization eliminates all such differences at once

Non-compliance

  • Common problem: people don’t always take the treatment they’re offered
    • Medical trials: patients skip the pills
    • Voucher experiments: families offered a voucher may never use it
  • We can only offer treatments, not force them
  • Does that ruin the experiment? No — we can adjust

Adjusting for non-compliance

  • Suppose half the offered families actually use the voucher
  • Raw earnings gap between offered and not-offered groups: $1,000
  • The whole gap comes from the half who moved, so the effect of using a voucher is $2,000

\text{True impact} = \frac{\text{Estimated impact}}{\text{Compliance rate}}

The Moving to Opportunity experiment

Affordable housing policy in the U.S.

  • The U.S. spends about $45 billion per year on housing vouchers, developer tax credits (LIHTC), and public housing
  • Standard economic theory predicts an equal-sized cash grant should beat any in-kind housing subsidy
  • Are housing policies actually effective at increasing mobility?
  • MTO lets us answer this with a real experiment

MTO: the design

  • Run 1994–1998 in five cities: Baltimore, Boston, Chicago, Los Angeles, New York
  • About 4,600 low-income families in public housing volunteered
  • Each family randomly assigned to one of three groups
  • (Chetty et al. 2016)

Where MTO families lived: New York

Source: Chetty, Hendren, and Katz 2016.

Three treatment arms

Group Offer Compliance
Experimental Voucher usable only in low-poverty (<10%) tracts 48% used it
Section 8 Conventional voucher, no location restriction 66% used it
Control No voucher; stayed eligible for public housing —
  • Random assignment means the three groups start out identical

The first verdict: “MTO failed”

  • Early research found little effect of moving on earnings
  • But it focused on adults and older youth at the time of the move (Kling et al. 2007)
  • Quasi-experimental work later suggested childhood exposure is what matters (Chetty and Hendren 2018)
  • New question: did MTO help the children who moved young?

A second look, powered by big data

  • Chetty et al. (2016) linked MTO participants to tax records
  • Tracked 99% of participants into their mid-20s
  • Split children by age at random assignment: below 13 vs. 13–18
  • Compare group averages, adjusting for compliance rates

  • College attendance (ages 18–20): 16.5% control → 21.7% experimental

What experiments can’t do

Limitation 1: attrition

  • Long-term evaluation requires tracking people for decades
  • Participants drop out — worse, dropout rates can differ across arms, destroying comparability
  • Big data largely fixes this: tax records let researchers follow 99% of MTO participants

Limitation 2: sample size

  • 4,600 families is small; estimates are imprecise
  • Big data does not fix this one — data got cheap, but running social experiments did not
  • Small samples are why MTO’s age patterns took so long to detect

Limitation 3: generalizability

  • MTO ran in five big cities in the 1990s
  • Do results carry to rural areas, other decades, other groups?
  • Quasi-experimental studies of 3 million movers fill these gaps: bigger samples, every U.S. neighborhood — at the price of stronger assumptions
  • Both approaches agree: moving to opportunity works for young children

Creating Moves to Opportunity

Voucher policy implication 1: timing

  • Vouchers are most valuable to families with young children — gains accumulate with years of exposure
  • Current U.S. practice of long waitlists is exactly backwards: families get help after children have aged
  • (Chetty et al. 2016)

Voucher policy implication 2: location

  • MTO’s unrestricted Section 8 arm produced smaller gains — even though those families could have made the same moves
  • Low-income families rarely use unrestricted transfers to change neighborhoods (Jacob et al. 2015)
  • Today, 80% of 2.1 million Section 8 vouchers are used in high-poverty, low-opportunity neighborhoods

  • Opportunity bargains exist — e.g., Normandy Park has rents similar to the Central District but far better outcomes for low-income kids

CMTO: a new experiment in Seattle

  • Pilot with the Seattle and King County housing authorities (Bergman et al. 2024)
  • Families already receiving vouchers randomly offered a support bundle:
    • Information about high-opportunity areas
    • Landlord recruitment in those areas
    • Personalized search assistance and short-term financial help
  • One reassurance for the design: tract-level mobility estimates are stable over time, so historical data can guide today’s families

CMTO: results

  • Share of families moving to high-opportunity areas: 15% → 53%
  • Cost: roughly $2,700 per family — tiny next to a projected lifetime earnings gain on the order of $200,000 per child
  • Follow-up work shows the customized assistance did the work, not cash
  • Lesson: families want opportunity; search barriers, not preferences, were holding them back

The big picture today

  • Neighborhood correlations reflect both sorting and causal effects — experiments let us isolate the causal part
  • MTO: moving young children to lower-poverty areas raised earnings, college attendance, and more
  • Effects work through childhood exposure — every year counts
  • Smart design (CMTO) can make existing voucher dollars far more effective

Why not run experiments for everything?

Three limits of randomized experiments

Even when an experiment is feasible and ethical, three limits remain:

  • Attrition — participants disappear over time, so long-term follow-up becomes unreliable — worst when attrition differs across treatment groups, because that breaks comparability itself
  • Sample size — experiments are expensive, so samples are small and estimates are noisy
  • Generalizability — five cities in the 1990s may not tell us about Columbus in 2026, and running experiments in every subgroup and area to “scale up” is impractical

Big data fixes exactly one of these

  • Attrition: largely fixed — linking MTO to tax records tracked 99% of participants
  • Sample size: not fixed — data got cheap; running experiments did not
  • Generalizability: not fixed — an experiment happens where it happens
  • So we need a second tool: the quasi-experiment

The movers design

The experiment we cannot run

  • Ideal: randomly assign children to neighborhoods at birth, then compare their adult outcomes
  • Obviously impossible — and unethical
  • A quasi-experiment gets as close to that ideal as observational data allows
  • The data: tax records on 3 million families that moved across Census tracts

Key idea: use the child’s age at the move

  • Families that move to different places are not comparable to each other
  • But families making the same move when the child is 5 vs. 15 are much more comparable
  • So: compare children whose families made the same move at different ages (Chetty and Hendren 2018)
  • The variation in age at move identifies childhood exposure effects

The age-at-move gradient

The younger the child at the move, the more their adult outcome converges to the destination

Source: Chetty and Hendren (2018)

Three lessons from that one figure

  1. Where you grow up really matters
  2. Childhood environment matters more than where you live as an adult
  3. Every extra year in a better place helps — outcomes converge about 4% per year of exposure

How the estimate actually works

  • Every tract has a known average outcome for children who grew up there from birth (the Opportunity Atlas)
  • Regress a mover child’s adult income on the destination-minus-origin difference in those averages
  • Run a separate regression for each age at move — one dot per age
  • The declining pattern of dots is the exposure effect

Is it really causal?

Every quasi-experiment leans on an assumption

  • The identification assumption: the timing of a family’s move is unrelated to other determinants of the child’s outcomes
  • If that holds, the age-at-move gradient is a causal exposure effect
  • Two ways it could fail — and a check for each

Threat 1: selection on timing

  • Families that move when their children are young may simply be different — more motivated, more resources
  • Their kids might have done better anywhere
  • If so, the gradient reflects families, not neighborhoods

Check 1: compare siblings

  • Siblings share the same parents and the same move — but different ages at the move
  • Within a family, the younger sibling gets more years of exposure
  • The same gradient shows up within families
  • So differences between moving families cannot be driving it

Threat 2: the move bundles other changes

  • Moves happen for reasons: a new job, a divorce, a raise
  • Maybe the reason for the move — not the new neighborhood — is what changes the child’s outcomes

Check 2: placebo tests using gender

  • Some areas produce better outcomes for boys than girls (for example, low-crime areas), and vice versa
  • Family moves to a “good for boys” place: the son improves in proportion to exposure — the daughter does not
  • A parent’s new job or a family shock should lift both children
  • Outcomes converge to the destination’s gender-specific pattern — hard to explain as anything but a real place effect

Bottom line

  • About two-thirds of the variation in upward mobility across areas is a causal effect of place
  • The remaining third is sorting
  • The Opportunity Atlas is not just a map of who lives where — places themselves shape children’s futures

What makes a good neighborhood?

The next question

  • Places causally shape children’s futures — but which features of places?
  • Approach: correlate upward mobility with area characteristics (Chetty et al. 2014)
  • These correlations are clues, not verdicts

  • Across the 30 largest metros, upward mobility is nearly unrelated to job growth

  • Booming cities often import talent rather than lift the kids who grow up there

The five strongest correlates

  1. Segregation — less segregation, more mobility
  2. Income inequality — bigger middle class, more mobility
  3. School quality — better schools, more mobility
  4. Family structure — more two-parent households, more mobility
  5. Social capital — more community connection, more mobility

1. Segregation

  • More segregation by race or income goes with less upward mobility
  • In highly segregated places, low-income children of all races fare worse
  • Possible channels: unequal resources (especially schools); fewer cross-group connections and mentors

Two maps, two futures

  • Sacramento’s neighborhoods are far more mixed — and its upward mobility is higher than Atlanta’s

2. Income inequality

  • Places with a smaller middle class have much less upward mobility
  • More inequality within a generation goes with less movement across generations

3. School quality

  • Higher spending, smaller classes, and higher test scores all go with more upward mobility
  • Schools are one obvious channel through which segregation could matter
  • Part 2 of this course tests school effects with causal designs

4. Family structure

  • The share of single-parent households is the strongest single predictor in the data
  • The striking part: it predicts worse outcomes even for children whose own parents are married
  • It is a feature of the community, not just of the child’s own family

5. Social capital

  • “It takes a village to raise a child” — will someone help you even when you are struggling?
  • Connections between low- and high-income people; Salt Lake City is the classic example
  • Putnam (1995) called its decline “Bowling Alone”

Handle these correlates with care

  • These are correlations across places — none is a proven cause
  • Example: we cannot conclude that marriage itself raises mobility
  • But they tell us where to look — and they shape the policies we study next

Policies and mobility

From evidence to policy

What we know coming into today

  • Neighborhoods have causal effects on children’s adult outcomes
  • The effects work through childhood exposure: every extra year in a better place helps, and gains are largest for young children
  • The experiment (MTO) and the quasi-experiment (3 million movers) agree (Chetty et al. 2016; Chetty and Hendren 2018)
  • Today: what should policy do with that knowledge?

Today’s plan

  1. Designing housing vouchers well
  2. Cost-benefit thinking: is any of this worth the money?
  3. Place-based investment: improving neighborhoods instead of leaving them
  4. Zooming out: the fading American Dream, at home and abroad

Designing housing vouchers

Lesson 1: target families with young children

  • Exposure effects mean the payoff is largest for the youngest movers
  • Current U.S. practice: join a waitlist — often up to 10 years long
  • Families sign up when kids are young and move when kids are older — exactly when moving helps least
  • Same dollars, much less benefit: a targeting failure, not a funding failure

Lesson 2: help families reach high-opportunity areas

  • In MTO, the unrestricted Section 8 voucher produced smaller gains than the experimental voucher — even though the same moves were allowed
  • Cash alone rarely leads families to lower-poverty neighborhoods (Jacob et al. 2015)
  • About 80% of the 2.1 million Section 8 vouchers are used in high-poverty, low-opportunity neighborhoods
  • Design matters: where the voucher gets used drives the benefit

Opportunity bargains

  • Central District: expensive and poor outcomes for low-income kids
  • Normandy Park: cheaper than the Central District, dramatically better outcomes
  • An opportunity bargain = affordable rent + high upward mobility
  • Vouchers can buy opportunity — families just need help finding it

Are the mobility estimates too old to use?

  • Atlas estimates come from children born in the early 1980s — is that still informative today?
  • Check: how well do old estimates predict newer cohorts’ outcomes?
  • Estimates that are 10 years old retain about 90% of the predictive power of one-year-old estimates
  • Neighborhood quality changes slowly — historical data are still useful

Creating Moves to Opportunity (CMTO)

  • Seattle pilot with families who already had vouchers (Bergman et al. 2024)
  • Three ingredients:
    1. Information for tenants about high-opportunity areas
    2. Landlord recruitment — simplify dealing with the housing authority
    3. Search assistance during the housing hunt
  • Light-touch services, large change in where families moved

Does it pass a cost-benefit test?

Method spotlight: cost-benefit analysis

  • Add up all costs and all benefits of a policy, whenever they occur — not just the upfront budget line
  • Benefits of moving children: higher adult earnings → higher tax revenue, plus lower spending on transfers and incarceration
  • Costs upfront, benefits decades later — big data lets us actually measure the later benefits

MTO children repaid the government

  • Annual income tax paid at age ≥ 24, children moved before age 13:
    • Control: $447
    • Section 8: $617
    • Experimental voucher: $841
  • Extra tax revenue alone recoups much of the voucher’s incremental cost over a working life

Three concerns about scaling vouchers up

  1. Cost — but vs. public housing, the incremental cost is negative: vouchers save taxpayers money in the long run
  2. Negative spillovers — does integration hurt the rich? Empirically, more integrated areas do not have worse outcomes for high-income kids
  3. Scalability — we cannot move everyone; if everyone moves, the “good neighborhood” doesn’t come with them

The scalability point is decisive

  • Moving families is a policy for some families, not all
  • At scale, we must make low-mobility neighborhoods better places to grow up
  • That is the case for place-based policy

What do high-mobility places look like?

Four strongest predictors of upward mobility across areas:

  1. Lower poverty rates (more mixed-income neighborhoods)
  2. More stable family structure (share of two-parent households)
  3. Greater social capital — connections across income groups
  4. Better school quality

Caution: these are correlations, not proven causes.

Opportunity is hyper-local

  • Control for a tract’s own poverty rate, and neighboring tracts’ poverty rates add almost no predictive power
  • Own-tract coefficient: −0.314; all ten nearest neighbors combined: −0.129
  • At the city-block level, only blocks within about 0.6 miles matter
  • Implication: interventions can work at the neighborhood scale — you don’t have to fix a whole city at once

Place-based policies in practice

  • Empowerment Zones (1990s): federal grants + hiring tax credits for designated distressed neighborhoods
  • Opportunity Zones (2017): capital-gains tax breaks for investment in designated low-income tracts
  • Hope VI: demolish distressed public housing, build mixed-income
  • Harlem Children’s Zone: cradle-to-career “pipeline” of schooling and family supports in a 97-block area

The hard evaluation question

  • A revitalized neighborhood is not the same as better-off people
  • If richer households move in and prior residents move out, the place improves while the people we meant to help gain nothing
  • Key question: do benefits reach prior residents, or just displace them?
  • Answering requires tracking people, not places — new Census linkages follow Americans born since the 1950s through these interventions

Building a pipeline of opportunity

  • Early childhood education → stable environment → mentorship → college and career readiness, with affordable housing throughout
  • Promising mentoring models: Becoming a Man, Credible Messengers, Peer Forward, the Harmony Project
  • Honest bottom line: we do not yet know which place-based investments are most effective — this is the research frontier

Why did the Dream fade? Two big changes

Since 1940, the U.S. economy changed in two first-order ways:

  1. Slower growth: total economic growth rates fell
  2. Less equally shared growth: more of the growth now goes to the top

Which change matters more? We can run the counterfactuals.

Growth used to be broadly shared

  • 1947–1973: real family incomes grew at a brisk, similar pace across the whole distribution — if anything, fastest at the bottom
  • 1973–2013: growth slowed overall and became steepest at the top

  • Two-thirds of the decline comes from the changed distribution of growth; one-third from slower growth

Is the U.S. experience universal?

The U.S. is an outlier twice over

  • Absolute mobility fell everywhere — this is not only an American story
  • But the U.S. decline is the largest (roughly 40% vs. ~20% in Denmark)
  • And its source differs: elsewhere, slowing growth explains most of the decline; in the U.S., rising inequality does
  • Denmark’s income shares barely moved since the 1950s — its entire decline is a growth story

Restoring the American Dream

  • Main lesson: reviving high absolute mobility requires more broadly shared growth — raising incomes at the bottom and middle
  • Two broad policy families:
    1. Redistribution: taxes, transfers, minimum wages (Piketty and Saez)
    2. Human capital: education keeping pace with technology (Goldin and Katz 2008)
  • “Education” includes social skills — the fastest wage growth is in high-social-skill occupations (Deming 2017)
  • Skill-building policies range from education and training programs to the housing-voucher policies we saw earlier

Three potential explanations

  1. Ability — parents who reached the top 1% may transmit talent
  2. Preferences — lower-income children may pick safer careers (doctor vs. start-up founder)
  3. Constraints / environment — comparable talent and preferences, but fewer resources and less exposure to innovation

What might be a better comparison to orange states?

Propensity-score reweighting

  • Orange states differ from the average blue state in many ways
  • Better: compare orange states to a weighted average of blue states that look most like them
  • The weight on each state is the propensity score
  • “Look like” means matching the distributions of characteristics
  • A crude (incorrect, but helpful) version: keep only blue states that border an orange state — that sets every other weight to 0

Reweighting the inventor data: a worked example

  • Suppose only two test scores exist: A and B
  • 300 low-income students: 150 A, 150 B
  • 300 high-income students: 200 A, 100 B
  • To mimic the high-income distribution, count each low-income A student twice as much as each B student
  • The reweighted average = the patent rate low-income kids would have if they had high-income kids’ grades

Result: scores explain less than a third of the gap

  • With the high-income score distribution, the patent-rate gap falls by 31%
  • So 3rd-grade scores account for 31% of the income gap in innovation — the other ~69% is something else
  • Environments already differ substantially by 3rd grade, so even that 31% overstates the role of innate ability

The arithmetic of a slow-closing gap

  • Among inventors born in 1940, about 8% were women
  • The female share rises only ~0.27 percentage points per year
  • At this pace, reaching a 50% female share takes about 118 years

Where we stand

  • Most of the innovation gap by income, race, and gender does not look like ability differences
  • But tests are imperfect measures of ability, and nothing so far is causal

Inventor-rich places produce inventors

  • San Jose: 5.4 inventors per 1,000 children; Minneapolis: 4.9; Madison: 4.3 — versus under 0.5 in the lowest areas
  • Children who grow up in CZs with many adult inventors are far more likely to become inventors themselves

Sorting or causation?

  • Consider two people who both live in Boston today:
  • One grew up in Silicon Valley → most likely to patent in computers
  • One grew up in Minneapolis (a medical-device hub) → most likely to patent in medical devices
  • Broad factors (school quality, resources) cannot explain patterns this specific — this points to mentors and role models

Exposure effects are gender-specific

Change in inventors per 1,000 children
Exposure to… Effect on boys Effect on girls
Male inventors +1.1 +0.1
Female inventors −0.2 +1.5
  • Boys respond to male inventors; girls respond to female inventors
  • If girls were as exposed to female inventors as boys are to male inventors, the gender gap in innovation would fall by half

Stereotypes form early

  • Bian et al. (2017) show children pictures of men and women and ask who is “really nice” and who is “really smart”
  • Age 5: no difference between boys and girls
  • Age 6: girls are much more likely to pick the man as “really smart”
  • At age 6, girls also start opting out of games “for children who are really, really smart”

A self-perpetuating gap

  • Few female inventors today → less exposure for girls → few female inventors tomorrow
  • Exposure effects make the gender gap reproduce itself across generations
  • Helps explain the crawl of 0.27 percentage points per year

Lost Einsteins

Lost Einsteins

  • Lost Einsteins: people who would have produced impactful inventions had they had exposure to innovation and resources in childhood
  • If women, minorities, and children from low-income families invented at the same rate as high-income white men, the U.S. innovation rate would quadruple
  • If innovation drives growth, this is a large cost to the whole economy

Mobility vs. growth: a false tradeoff?

  • We opened with a worry: more mobility might come at the cost of growth
  • The Lost Einsteins evidence points the other way
  • Talent is broadly distributed; opportunity is not — so misallocated talent shrinks the innovation pipeline
  • Helping people reach better outcomes and growing the economy can be the same policy
