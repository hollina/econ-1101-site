Education and mobility

Education: the classic mobility engine

  • Education is widely viewed as the most scalable pathway to upward mobility — but is it still one in the U.S.?
  • U.S. students score below many peer countries despite higher spending on schools
  • Sharp differences in school quality across districts
  • Rising college costs → access problems for low-income students
  • Some colleges (e.g., for-profits) may not produce good outcomes

Could colleges make mobility worse?

  • In principle, college is a path up that doesn’t depend on your childhood ZIP code
  • But if children from higher-income families attend better colleges, higher education may not promote mobility
  • If attendance gaps are large enough, colleges could actually increase the persistence of income across generations
  • Which one it is — engine or bottleneck — is an empirical question
  • Core source today: Chetty, Friedman, Saez, Turner, and Yagan (2017), “Mobility Report Cards”

Three ingredients

How the higher education system affects mobility depends on:

  1. Inputs — parental income distribution at each college: who gets in?
  2. Outputs — students’ earnings conditional on parent income at each college: who moves up?
  3. Causal share — how much of the earnings variation colleges actually cause (vs. selection)

The data

  • Every college student in the U.S., 1999–2013 — about 30 million students
  • Parent and student income from tax records
  • College attendance from 1098-T tuition forms and Pell grant records; test scores from the College Board
  • All statistics based on attendance, not completion

Who goes where: access

Measuring parents’ incomes

  • Parent income: average pre-tax household income during the five years when the child is 15–19
  • Converted to percentile ranks among parents of children in the same birth cohort
  • Benchmarks for the 1980 cohort: p20 ≈ $25k · median ≈ $60k · p80 ≈ $111k · p99 ≈ $512k

Are colleges more integrated than neighborhoods?

  • Common perception: college mixes students from different backgrounds more than the places they grew up
  • Data say: income segregation across colleges ≈ segregation across childhood ZIP codes
  • For a bottom-quintile student, the peer distribution in college looks about like the childhood neighborhood’s
  • Top-quintile students at Ivy-Plus colleges have fewer low- and middle-income peers than they did growing up

Has access improved over time?

  • 2000–2011: income segregation across colleges roughly unchanged
  • 2006–07: elite privates became effectively free for low- and middle-income families — yet no dramatic rise in low-income shares
  • Budget-squeezed state flagships raise tuition and recruit out-of-state (richer) students → serve fewer low-income students
  • More low-income students shifted to for-profits and some two-year colleges
  • Lesson: affordability is necessary but not sufficient

Outcomes: who moves up

Measuring students’ outcomes

  • Children’s individual earnings in their mid-30s — avoids patterns driven by marriage rates rather than own earnings
  • Ranked against everyone born in the same year
  • 1980 cohort at age 34: p50 ≈ $28k · p80 ≈ $58k · p99 ≈ $197k

The striking within-college result

  • At virtually every college, students from low- and high-income families reach the top quintile at similar rates
  • Most of the low- vs. high-income outcome gap is between colleges, not within them
  • Columbia and SUNY-Stony Brook students end up in different places — but within each campus, parent income barely predicts outcomes
  • So reallocating students across colleges could move mobility a lot; if within-college gaps were large, admissions reform would accomplish little

Mobility report cards

A report card in one number

  • Access: fraction of a college’s students from the bottom quintile
  • Success rate: fraction of those students who reach the top quintile
  • Mobility rate (MR) = Access × Success rate — the joint probability of starting in the bottom fifth and reaching the top fifth
  • SUNY-Stony Brook: 16% × 51% = 8.4%

TopHat: Compute a mobility rate

At College X, 5% of students come from the bottom quintile, and 60% of those students reach the top quintile. What is X’s mobility rate?

  • A: 5% · B: 3% · C: 60% · D: 12%
  • Access × success rate: 0.05 × 0.60 = 3%
  • Note what MR punishes: superb outcomes can’t produce mobility if almost no low-income students are enrolled

Two levers for policy

  • The engines of mobility are mid-tier public institutions — Cal State LA, CUNY, Stony Brook, UTEP — not the Ivies
  • Lever 1: raise access at colleges with excellent outcomes (Harvard, Michigan)
  • Lever 2: raise success at high-access colleges — help students persist and finish
  • But both levers assume colleges cause outcomes — do they?

Method spotlight: regression discontinuity

Causation or selection?

  • Harvard students are highly selected — they would likely earn a lot wherever they enrolled
  • Pure selection → reallocating students across colleges changes nothing
  • Causal effects → admissions policy is a powerful mobility lever
  • The ideal experiment: randomly assign students to colleges, compare earnings at age 30
  • ~3,000 colleges → we’d need ~3,000 experiments. Infeasible — so we hunt for quasi-experiments

The Florida rule: SAT below 970 → GPA of at least 3.0

Source: Zimmerman (2014)

Anatomy of a regression discontinuity

  • Running variable: distance of your GPA from the cutoff that applies to you
  • Cutoff: GPA 3.0 (for students with SAT below 970)
  • GPA 3.01 → treatment group; GPA 2.99 → control group
  • Setting: Florida International University (FIU), the least selective four-year school in the state system
  • Control-group students typically attend a two-year community college instead

The cutoff really moves attendance

23.4 pp (2.1 pp) 10.4 pp (2.5 pp)

Source: Zimmerman (2014)

Sharp vs. fuzzy regression discontinuity

  • Sharp RD: the probability of treatment jumps from 0 to 100% at the cutoff — the rule is followed mechanically
  • Fuzzy RD: the probability of treatment increases at the cutoff, but not from 0 to 100%
  • Zimmerman’s design is fuzzy: attendance rises only 10.4 pp at the cutoff — some students below the cutoff still get in, and some above it go elsewhere

The identification assumption

  • All other determinants of earnings are balanced on either side of the cutoff — students at 2.99 and 3.01 are comparable
  • Then any jump in earnings at the threshold must come from the jump in the chance of attending FIU
  • Plausible here because the cutoff was not publicized
  • If it were well known, students might manipulate the running variable — extra effort or savvy counselors nudging GPAs just over 3.0 — breaking comparability

Balance check: gender

Checking covariate balance

  • Test the assumption: plot predetermined characteristics around the cutoff
  • Share of Black students ≈ 30% on both sides — no jump; gender shares similar too
  • A discontinuity in any predetermined characteristic would mean we’re not comparing similar people — the design would fail

The payoff: earnings jump at the cutoff

$372 or 5.1% (s.e. $141)

Source: Zimmerman (2014)

TopHat: Who does this estimate apply to?

Zimmerman finds a 5.1% earnings gain. For whom is this the causal effect of attending FIU?

  • A: all FIU students · B: all Florida graduates · C: students with GPAs near the cutoff · D: everyone at community colleges
  • RD delivers a local estimate: students near the cutoff
  • A caveat — and a feature: these are exactly the marginal students an admissions change would move

When RD can go wrong

Challenges to “identifying” causal treatment effects with a regression discontinuity:

  • The assignment rule is known in advance
  • There is an incentive to adjust the running variable
  • There is time to adjust the running variable
  • The cutoff is endogenous — tied to other things that shift the potential outcomes
  • There is nonrandom “heaping” along the running variable

From one cutoff to 3,000 colleges

  • We can’t find a clean cutoff for every college
  • Shortcut: compare earnings controlling for SAT scores and parent income as each college’s causal-effect estimate
  • Validation: at FIU, this control-based estimate matches the quasi-experimental RD estimate
  • Takeaway: a meaningful share of cross-college earnings differences reflects causal effects, not just selection

Getting talent in the door

Undermatching

  • Many high-achieving, low-income students never apply to selective colleges (Hoxby and Avery 2013)
  • It’s not mainly sticker price: with financial aid, the net cost of top colleges for low-income families is often below that of less selective schools
  • More plausible barriers: information and application support (Hoxby and Turner 2013)
  • Testable with an experiment — enter HAIL

The HAIL experiment

  • Dynarski, Libassi, Michelmore, and Owen (2018), University of Michigan
  • Target: high-achieving (GPA > 3.3, SAT > 1100) students from low-income families (incomes below $47k, proxied by free/reduced-price lunch)
  • Found via statewide administrative data on GPAs, SAT scores (mandatory in Michigan), and lunch eligibility — about 2,000 students per year qualify
  • Random assignment: 50% receive the HAIL treatment, 50% control

The treatment: an early, certain promise

The HAIL Scholarship mailer sent to treated students — Source: Dynarski et al. (2018)

What the mailer promised

  • Personalized mailings to students and parents
  • Four years of free tuition (~$60,000), guaranteed up front; application fees waived
  • Made aid these students already qualified for certain and salient

The letter parents received

“Since your child is an excellent student, we want to offer a potentially transformative college opportunity: If [your child] applies and is admitted to the University of Michigan – Ann Arbor, your child will be awarded the HAIL Scholarship covering the entire cost of U-M tuition and fees for four years… worth approximately $60,000.”

  • Signed by the Associate VP for Enrollment Management — a concrete, personal promise, not a generic aid brochure
  • And a nudge: after the financial aid application, students would likely qualify for additional aid for housing and textbooks

HAIL results

Outcome Control Treatment
Applied to Michigan 26% 67%
Admitted 15% 32%
Enrolled 12% 27%
  • Enrollment more than doubled — a ~15-pp jump from a cheap, low-touch mailing (Dynarski et al. 2018)
  • Students remained enrolled two years later; many would otherwise have attended community college

What HAIL solves — and what it can’t

  • Removing informational and cost-certainty barriers substantially raises access for high-achieving, low-income students
  • But the scale is small: roughly 300 extra students per year
  • That will not desegregate the higher education system by itself
  • Even hypothetical pure SAT-based admissions would barely change low-income shares at elite colleges

Recap

  • Access is deeply unequal: a top-1% child is 103× more likely to attend Harvard than a bottom-quintile child
  • Within colleges, outcomes by parent income are similar — the action is between colleges
  • MR = access × success: mid-tier publics (Cal State LA, CUNY, Stony Brook) are America’s mobility engines
  • Zimmerman’s RD: four-year vs. community college → +5.1% earnings for marginal students
  • HAIL: an early, certain tuition-free promise doubled enrollment

A $1 trillion system

Are test scores real?

Test scores: the big data of education

  • Standardized scores exist for virtually every student, every year
  • Already recorded in administrative databases — cheap at scale
  • Observed immediately, unlike college-going or earnings at 30
  • The workhorse output of the education production function
  • Inputs we control: class size, teachers, technology — limited by a budget

The obvious worry

  • Are score gains learning — or just test-taking skill?
  • A teacher can “teach to the test”: scores rise, life skills don’t
  • If scores are hollow, every score-based evaluation is hollow too
  • The fix: link childhood scores to adult outcomes
  • Chetty et al. (2011): 12,000 Tennessee kindergartners from 1985, school records linked to tax records

What a kindergarten test looks like

  • Word-and-picture questions, read aloud. Example:
    • “I’ll say a word — cup. Listen for the ending sound. Circle the picture that starts with that sound.”
  • Scores converted to percentiles: each child’s rank (0-100) among all test takers
  • Can a test like this possibly predict adult earnings?

Mapping every district in America

Test scores as a national yardstick

  • With scores for nearly every student, we can compare performance across schools and subgroups (e.g., poor vs. rich)
  • Problem: states give different tests, in different grades — raw scores aren’t comparable across district lines
  • Reardon et al. (2016): 215 million test scores from 11,000 school districts, grades 3-8, 2009-13 — placed on one national scale

Making scores comparable across the U.S.

  1. Rank each district’s average score within its state’s distribution (for a given grade, year, and subject)
  2. Use a national test the Dept. of Education gives a sample of students to convert state ranks to a national scale
    • If CA scores 5 percentiles below the national average, a district 10 percentiles below the CA mean is 15 below the national mean
  3. Convert mean scores to grade-level equivalents — “this district is 1.5 grade levels behind”

How can we improve poorly performing schools?

  • Districts like Palo Alto and Cambridge score far above the national average; districts like Detroit score far below
  • Many districts sit two grade levels behind the national average even controlling for socioeconomic status — an SES index built from Census data (income, college share, single-parent rates)
  • Simply spending more money is not obviously the solution — recall the PISA chart
  • We need to know which inputs matter. First up: class size

The Tennessee STAR experiment

Why not just compare small vs. large classes?

  • In observational data, students in small classes differ: richer districts, better-funded schools, different families
  • The naive comparison overstates the benefit of small classes
  • Same selection problem as neighborhoods and colleges
  • The cleanest fix: randomize class assignment

STAR: the design

  • Student/Teacher Achievement Ratio experiment, Tennessee, 1985-89
  • About 12,000 children, grades K-3, at 79 mostly disadvantaged schools
  • Students and teachers randomly assigned to classrooms within each school
  • Small class ≈ 15 students; regular class ≈ 22
  • Randomization → groups comparable on average → differences in outcomes are causal

Aside: what does the (1.05) mean?

  • Rerun the experiment and you would not get 4.81 every time
  • The standard error quantifies that chance variation
  • 95% confidence interval ≈ estimate ± 1.96 × SE
  • Test scores: 4.81 ± 1.96 × 1.05 → roughly +3 to +7 percentiles
  • That interval excludes zero — we are confident the effect is real

TopHat: the −$4 earnings estimate

STAR’s earnings estimate is −$4 (SE = $327). What should we conclude?

  • A: Small classes don’t affect earnings
  • B: Small classes lower earnings by $4
  • C: Too noisy to tell — the 95% CI runs from about −$640 to +$637 a year
  • Against a $15,912 mean, that CI includes earnings gains up to 4%

Fade-out… and re-emergence

  • The test-score gains from small classes fade: mostly gone by middle school
  • Yet decades later, effects re-emerge in adult outcomes — college attendance and earnings-related measures
  • Leading explanation: lasting non-cognitive skills — effort, initiative, behavior
  • Lesson: don’t judge early education by test scores alone

STAR’s limitation

  • 12,000 kids is huge for an experiment — tiny for detecting earnings effects
  • Experiments at this scale are rare and expensive
  • Alternative: find a quasi-experiment hiding in administrative data on millions of students
  • Enter a rule from the 12th century…

Maimonides’ rule

A 12th-century class-size cap

  • Maimonides (1138-1204): once a class passes 40 students, it must be split and a second teacher appointed
  • Israel still caps classes at 40 — Angrist and Lavy (1999) named the resulting research design “Maimonides’ rule”
  • Sweden uses the same idea with a cap of 25 (Fredriksson, Öckert, and Oosterbeek 2013)
  • Caps create sudden changes in class size that families don’t control

Caps create a natural experiment

  • Sweden: maximum class size of 25 students per grade
  • A grade with 25 students → one class of 25
  • A grade with 26 students → two classes of 13
  • One extra enrollee slashes average class size
  • Schools that happen to have 25 vs. 26 students in a grade are otherwise essentially identical

TopHat: who is “treated” here?

One Swedish school has 26 fourth-graders; a nearby school has 25. Which school’s students are the treatment group?

  • A: The school with 25 — its single class is bigger
  • B: The school with 26 — crossing the cap put its students in much smaller classes
  • C: Neither — nothing was randomly assigned
  • Compare to last class: same logic as the GPA cutoff, new running variable

Why believe the jump is causal?

  • Key assumption: schools just above and just below the cutoff are comparable — the only thing that changes at 26 is class size
  • Checks, same as last class:
    • Covariate balance: student characteristics smooth through the cutoff
    • No manipulation: families don’t sort around a grade-enrollment threshold
  • Enrollment counts are hard for any one family to game — a strong RD

Is it worth the money?

From 4% to dollars: present value

  • Child from a family at the 25th percentile: about $1.3 million in lifetime earnings
  • Money earned decades from now is worth less than money today — discounting gives a present value ≈ $500,000
  • A 4% earnings gain from one year in a smaller class ≈ $20,000 per child in present value

The back-of-the-envelope

  • Split a class of 30 into two classes of 15
  • 30 students × $20,000 ≈ $600,000+ in higher lifetime earnings
  • Cost: one extra teacher and one extra room for a year — far below $600,000
  • Early-childhood investment can pay for itself many times over
  • Echoes a broader lesson: returns to investing in children are highest early

Does it scale?

  • These estimates are partial equilibrium: change one child’s class, hold the rest of the world fixed
  • If every class shrinks, do the gains survive?
  • Pure competition for fixed slots (a seat at Harvard): gains crowd each other out
  • Real skill-building (human capital): new productivity, not a zero-sum contest
  • Evidence from large schooling expansions: crowd-out is small

Recap

  • KG test scores strongly predict adult outcomes — but explain only 5% of earnings variation
  • Reardon et al.: 215 million scores on one national scale — districts differ by multiple grade levels, even holding SES fixed
  • STAR: random assignment → small classes raise scores (+4.8 percentiles) and college-going (+2 pp); earnings too noisy
  • Score gains fade out, then effects re-emerge in adulthood
  • Maimonides-rule cutoffs: 5 fewer students → +0.2 SD scores, +4% adult earnings
  • Smaller early classes likely pass a cost-benefit test — ~$20,000 per child vs. modest costs

Teachers and charter schools

Today’s question

Do great teachers change students’ lives — and can we tell who they are from data?

  • Last class: class size matters. Today: the people in the classroom
  • Then the other paradigm: can market competition — charter schools — improve education?
  • Reading: Chetty, Friedman, and Rockoff (2014), Measuring the Impacts of Teachers II

Where we’re headed

  • Teacher value-added (VA): measuring teacher quality from test-score gains
  • The three-part debate over using VA for policy
  • Method spotlight: the event study — our newest quasi-experimental tool
  • What a high-VA teacher is worth: college, earnings, and more
  • Charter schools: lottery evidence and the limits of markets

Teacher value-added

Using big data to study teachers’ impacts

  • Chetty, Friedman, and Rockoff (2014): New York City school district records, 1989–2009
  • 2.5 million children and 18 million test scores
  • Linked to tax records: earnings, college attendance, teenage birth rates
  • Big data lets us follow students from a 4th-grade classroom into their late 20s

Measuring teacher quality: value-added

  • Teacher value-added (VA): how much a teacher raises her students’ test scores on average
  • For a 4th-grade teacher: students’ average score at the end of 4th grade minus their average score at the end of 3rd grade
  • Adjusted for noise and for differences in student characteristics
  • Students consistently gain a lot → high-VA teacher

TopHat: Which teacher has higher value-added?

Ms. A’s students end the year at the 80th percentile, right where they started. Ms. B’s students started at the 30th percentile and end at the 45th.

  • A: Ms. A — her students score higher
  • B: Ms. B — her students gained more
  • C: Can’t tell without more information
  • Remember: VA is about gains, not levels

The debate over teacher VA

  • Using VA to evaluate, pay, or fire teachers is controversial and highly politicized
  • At its core, the fight is about three statistical issues:
    1. Bias — do VA differences capture teachers’ causal impacts, or just which students they are assigned? (sorting)
    2. Long-term impacts — do high-VA teachers improve lives, or just “teach to the test”?
    3. Instability — are estimates from a few classes too noisy to use for policy?

Method spotlight: the event study

The ideal experiment

  • Use historical data to label teachers high-VA or low-VA
  • Then randomly assign a fresh set of students to those teachers
  • If VA is a real causal measure, students assigned to high-VA teachers should do better
  • No district will let us run this — so we approximate it

The quasi-experiment: teacher turnover

  • Teachers switch schools a lot
  • From the students’ point of view, when a great teacher arrives at their school is essentially random
  • Question: when a high-VA teacher enters a school, do that grade’s scores jump?
  • The design that answers this is called an event study

Entry of a high-VA teacher

Source: Chetty, Friedman, and Rockoff (2014)

Reading the high-VA entry

  • Track one grade in one school across school years
  • A top-5% VA teacher arrives at the end of 1995
  • 4th-grade scores jump immediately — and stay high as she teaches later cohorts

The control group hiding in the picture

  • Worry: maybe the school improved in other ways at the same moment
  • Check: 3rd-grade scores in the same school — those students never had the new teacher
  • They stay flat right through the entry year
  • Sharper still: a better math teacher raises math scores, not English scores

Entry of a low-VA teacher

Source: Chetty, Friedman, and Rockoff (2014)

Reading the low-VA entry

  • Same design, opposite event: a bottom-5% VA teacher arrives
  • 4th-grade scores fall immediately relative to the prior grade
  • Across the whole VA distribution, effects are roughly linear: the best teachers help most, the worst hurt most

Anatomy of an event study

  1. Define the event: the year the new teacher enters = year 0
  2. Re-center time for every event: −2, −1, 0, +1, +2, …
  3. Stack roughly 2,000 such entry events on top of each other
  4. Average the outcome at each event time
  • The comparison is the change around the event — not levels across schools

Why not just compare across schools?

  • Schools that attract high-VA teachers differ from schools that don’t — in many ways at once
  • A cross-school comparison mixes teacher effects with everything else
  • The event study looks within the same school, at the moment of entry
  • The pre-event years are the “before” photo

TopHat: When should you distrust an event study?

Suppose 4th-grade scores had already been rising for three years before the high-VA teacher arrived. Why is that a problem?

  • A: It isn’t — scores went up, so the teacher worked
  • B: Something else was already improving the school, so the jump can’t be credited to the teacher
  • C: The sample is too small
  • Flat pre-trends are the credibility check for every event study

Event studies are everywhere: leaded fuel in NASCAR

  • Ordinary cars gave up leaded gasoline decades ago — but automotive racing was still allowed to use it, and switched to unleaded in 2007 (5.2 g of lead per gallon → none)
  • One race: 40 cars, up to 500 miles, at 2–5 mpg — roughly 45–110 pounds of lead emitted
  • For scale: an average airport emits about 100 pounds per year

Teachers’ long-run impacts

Lesson 1: VA estimates are essentially unbiased

  • Assigned to a teacher whose measured VA is 10 percentiles higher → your score rises by about 10 percentiles
  • So VA measured in ordinary observational data mostly reflects causal effects of teachers, not student sorting
  • Debate issue #1 — bias — turns out to be small

College attendance

Source: Chetty, Friedman, and Rockoff (2014)

College attendance: what it means

  • One year with a higher-VA elementary teacher → about 2 percentage points more likely to attend college
  • Attendance at age 20 rises from roughly 36% (5th-percentile teacher) to 38.5% (95th-percentile teacher)
  • That’s one teacher, one year — effects add up across grades

Earnings

Source: Chetty, Friedman, and Rockoff (2014)

Earnings: what it means

  • Earnings at age 28 rise steadily with the VA of your elementary school teacher: roughly $20.5K → $22K from the 5th to the 95th percentile
  • Noisier than college attendance — earnings vary a lot across people — but clearly upward

Teenage births

Source: Chetty, Friedman, and Rockoff (2014)

Teenage births: what it means

  • Girls assigned to low-VA elementary teachers are significantly more likely to have a teenage birth
  • The rate falls from about 14.5% to 12.5% moving from the 5th to the 95th VA percentile
  • Teachers shape outcomes far beyond the test

Lesson 2: it’s not teaching to the test

  • Teachers who raise test scores also raise college attendance and earnings, and reduce teenage births
  • Test-score-based VA predicts long-term impacts
  • Debate issue #2 resolved: high-VA teachers improve lives, not just scores

What is a better teacher worth?

  • Replace one bottom-5% teacher with an average teacher, for one classroom, for one year:
  • Each child’s lifetime earnings rise by about $80,000 — a couple thousand dollars a year over 40 years
  • ≈ $2.2 million per classroom of 28 students, undiscounted
  • Discounting future earnings at 5%: present value ≈ $407,000 per classroom

Lesson 3: a few years of data is enough

  • Debate issue #3: with few classes, teachers who get lucky look great, and vice versa
  • With limited data, some decent (25th–30th percentile) teachers do get misclassified into the bottom 5% —
  • but essentially no above-median teacher lands there by chance after about three years
  • Achievable gain with 2–3 years of data: about $250,000 of the $407,000 perfect-information benchmark

Policy implications

  • Status quo: 98%+ of public school teachers get tenure within three years; pay is set by experience, not performance
  • Forced to choose, better teachers likely beat smaller classes — though magnitudes matter
  • Use VA alongside other measures: classroom observation, principal ratings, student evaluations
  • Attracting and retaining top teachers in disadvantaged schools may be one of the highest-return mobility policies we have

This research reached the very top

“We know a good teacher can increase the lifetime income of a classroom by over $250,000…. Every person in this chamber can point to a teacher who changed the trajectory of their lives.”

— Barack Obama, State of the Union, 2012

  • Mayor Bloomberg’s 2012 State of the City cited the same study: students with effective teachers are more likely to go to college and to get higher-paying jobs — and he argued the city could not keep ineffective teachers in classrooms

Charter schools

Two policy paradigms

  • Government-based solutions: improve public schools directly — smaller classes, better teachers (everything so far today)
  • Market-based solutions: introduce choice and competition — charter schools and vouchers
  • A contentious policy debate — but one the data can referee

Charters and vouchers, defined

  • Charter school: publicly funded, but run independently of the public school system
  • Voucher: your tax dollars follow the student — usable at any school you choose, including private schools
  • The market logic: good schools attract students; bad schools must improve or close

The selection problem (again)

  • We can’t just compare charter vs. public school outcomes
  • Charters open disproportionately in low-income, disadvantaged urban areas
  • Raw comparisons confound school effects with student backgrounds
  • Solution: oversubscribed charters admit by lottery — a built-in randomized experiment

Lottery evidence: Boston charters vs. pilots

Source: Abdulkadiroǧlu, Angrist, Dynarski, Kane, and Pathak (2011)

Reading the lottery estimates

  • Compare lottery winners vs. losers (Abdulkadiroǧlu, Angrist, Dynarski, Kane, and Pathak 2011)
  • Charters — exempt from district regulations and union contracts: winners do much better, including higher college attendance (Angrist et al. 2013)
  • Pilot schools — charter-like, but bound by the regulations and contracts, and financed the same way: no gain
  • The charters’ flexibility (e.g., in hiring) appears to be what matters

“No excuses” schools

  • Nationwide, charters average small positive test-score effects — with enormous variability across schools (Chabrier, Cohodes, and Oreopoulos 2016)
  • The standout type: “no excuses” charters (e.g., KIPP) — academically rigorous, extra hours
  • The name: teachers have no excuse for any child underperforming
  • These show the most positive impacts of any charter type

Does market discipline improve schools over time?

Markets prune the worst charters

  • Texas (Baude, Casey, Hanushek, and Rivkin 2014): in 2001, charter VA was widely dispersed, and the average charter was slightly worse than the average public school
  • The worst charters — one standard deviation or more below — have since closed, and better schools (including “no excuses” charters) have gained enrollment
  • The charter distribution shifts up and tightens, as markets predict — but some low-performing schools persist even many years after the system began

TopHat: Why compare charters to pilot schools?

Boston charters and pilots both admit by lottery, so selection is handled in both. What does adding pilots to the study teach us?

  • A: Nothing — it’s just a robustness check
  • B: It isolates which feature of charters matters: exemption from regulations and union contracts
  • C: It shows admission lotteries don’t work

Three limits of the market approach

  1. Markets need observable quality — but true school quality (long-run value-added) takes years to see
  2. Cream skimming — schools have incentives to reject weaker applicants, concentrating disadvantage in public schools with fewer resources and weaker peers
  3. Markets need good choosers — when Charlotte, NC introduced school choice in 2002, low-income parents were much less likely than high-income parents to choose high-scoring schools (Hastings, Kane, and Staiger 2007) — so choice can amplify achievement gaps

So: markets or government?

  • A strict market approach might raise the average but deliver very unequal outcomes across schools and subpopulations
  • Public-school constraints — like rigid hiring rules — clearly have costs too
  • Chetty’s view: a hybrid may be best — charter-style flexibility within schools, uniform quality and resources across schools

Recap

  • Teacher VA = average test-score gains, adjusted; the debate = bias, long-run impacts, instability
  • Event study: stack entry events at time 0, look for a jump with flat pre-trends; the untaught grade is the control
  • VA is essentially unbiased, and a high-VA teacher raises college and earnings and lowers teen births — ≈ $250K per classroom in present value
  • Charters: lottery evidence shows big gains at flexible, “no-excuses” schools — but markets have real limits
