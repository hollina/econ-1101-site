Tax policy I: income taxation and behavioral responses

Before we start

Today’s question

How high should income tax rates be — especially on top earners?

  • Sounds like a purely political question
  • Economics can’t pick the “right” answer, but it can structure the tradeoff — and big data can measure the key ingredient
  • Method today: supply and demand, plus elasticity

Where we’re headed

  • How governments actually raise money
  • Marginal vs. average tax rates; a century of top rates
  • The equity–efficiency tradeoff and the Laffer curve
  • Evidence: Denmark’s 1987 reform, Kansas’s 2012 experiment, and bunching at kinks
  • Reading: Diamond and Saez (2011) — the case for a progressive tax

How governments raise money

Federal taxes, 1960 vs. 2014

Source: Gruber (2016)

What changed between 1960 and 2014

  • Income taxes: the workhorse, roughly stable at ~40–44%
  • Corporate taxes shrank (23% → 15%); social insurance contributions more than doubled (17% → 35%)

Still true in 2022

Source: Tax Foundation (2024)

The shrinking corporate share

  • Corporate share keeps falling — now just 6.5%
  • Very profitable firms often pay little via avoidance — a live policy debate (think of the Amazon headlines)

The U.S. is unusual

Source: Gruber (2008)

Why other countries tax differently

  • Most countries lean much harder on consumption taxes (VAT)
  • Mexico: ~74% from consumption — income taxes are hard to enforce without excellent records
  • The U.S. reliance on income taxation is the outlier

Reading a progressive tax

Progressive income taxation

  • Most developed countries have progressive income tax systems
  • Tax rates rise with income → the rich pay a larger share of their income than the poor
  • Implemented with a set of tax brackets based on income
  • To read a bracket system correctly you need one crucial distinction…

Marginal is not average

Marginal vs. average federal tax rates for a married couple, 2019 schedule

Reading the two rates

  • Marginal rate: tax on your next dollar — a step function
  • Average rate: total tax ÷ total income — rises smoothly, always below the marginal rate here

TopHat: Does a raise into the 22% bracket cut your take-home pay?

You earn just below a bracket cutoff at a 12% marginal rate. A raise pushes you $1,000 over the line into the 22% bracket. Your after-tax income…

  • A: falls — the higher rate now applies to all your income
  • B: rises — only the $1,000 above the cutoff is taxed at 22%
  • C: stays exactly the same

Why top rates get all the attention

  • Income is heavily concentrated: the top 1% earn close to 20% of total income
  • So the top marginal rate is a huge revenue lever for the government
  • It is also the flashpoint of the inequality debate — and it has swung wildly over U.S. history

A century of top tax rates

The rollercoaster in words

  • Around 1950: top rate ≈ 90% — earn an extra $1,000 at the top, keep $100
  • The 1980s Reagan cuts brought ~70% down to today’s ~35–40%

Equity vs. efficiency

The equity force: diminishing marginal utility

  • Tax rates are usually debated as pure politics; the economic approach weighs equity against efficiency
  • Equity: an extra $1 is worth more to a family earning $10,000 than to one earning $250,000
  • If dollars matter more in poorer hands, a more equal distribution raises total well-being
  • This force pushes toward higher tax rates on high earners

The efficiency force: incentives

  • Higher top rates → less incentive for high earners to work, start businesses, innovate
  • At a 90–95% rate, why launch the company at all?
  • Less entrepreneurship and growth hurts everyone
  • This force pushes toward lower tax rates on high earners

What data can — and cannot — settle

  • How much more is a dollar worth to a poor family? A value judgment — economists leave it to the public and politics
  • How much less do people work when tax rates rise? A measurable quantity
  • That quantity is the elasticity of labor supply — the centerpiece of a large empirical literature using tax data

Method spotlight: supply, demand, and elasticity

The labor supply curve

Reading the labor supply curve

  • Vertical axis: hourly wage; horizontal axis: hours people choose to work
  • Example: at $10/hour people work 35 hours; at $20/hour, 40 hours
  • An income tax lowers the take-home wage — so the slope of this curve tells us how much taxes matter

Elasticity: responsiveness in one number

  • Elasticity = % change in quantity when price changes by 1%

\varepsilon \;=\; \frac{\%\ \Delta\ \text{hours worked}}{\%\ \Delta\ \text{wage}}

  • Unit-free — comparable across countries, years, and datasets
  • If \varepsilon = 0.5: a 10% wage increase → people work 5% more

Flat or steep supply?

Why the slope is the whole debate

  • Less elastic (steep): tax the wage, hours barely change — small efficiency cost
  • More elastic (flat): small tax changes move hours a lot — big efficiency cost
  • The whole equity–efficiency debate hinges on this one parameter

  • B: 0.3
  • C: 3.3
  • D: 10

The Laffer curve

A thought experiment

  • Suppose we place zero social value on additional income for the top 1%
  • Surely then the optimal top tax rate is 100%?
  • No — even with zero weight on the rich, the revenue-maximizing rate is below 100%
  • The reason is the Laffer curve

The Laffer curve

The Laffer curve

Why the curve bends back

  • Revenue R(t) = t \times L(t): the rate times the tax base
  • At t = 0: no revenue. At t = 100\%: nobody works — the take-home wage is zero — so also no revenue
  • Inverted U → some revenue-maximizing rate t_{max} in between
  • Above t_{max} is the wrong side: cutting rates would raise revenue

The revenue-maximizing rate

  • Maximizing R(t) = t \times L(t) gives a remarkably simple formula:

t^{*} \;=\; \frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}

Elasticity \varepsilon Revenue-maximizing rate
0 100%
0.25 80%
1 50%
  • More responsive workers → a lower revenue-maximizing rate

Napkin economics: the supply-side movement

  • Arthur Laffer famously sketched the curve on a restaurant napkin in 1974; the idea powered the “supply-side” movement
  • Claim: the U.S. was on the wrong side — cutting top rates would raise revenue and stimulate growth
  • Motivated the large Reagan-era top tax cuts
  • This is a testable empirical claim — so let’s test it

Testing the Laffer logic with data

How do you estimate the elasticity?

  • Modern studies use difference-in-differences on tax reforms
  • A reform changes rates for one group (say, top earners) but not others
  • Other income groups → the control group
  • The best studies use Scandinavian administrative data — better income records than the U.S.

Denmark’s 1987 reform

Why the reform is a clean experiment

  • 1984 marginal rates: top bracket ≈ 72%, middle ≈ 62%, bottom ≈ 48%
  • 1987: sharp cuts for the top and middle brackets — bottom bracket left unchanged
  • Treatment groups + untouched control group = clean difference-in-differences

What happened to taxable income

Reading the difference-in-differences

  • Before 1987 the three groups move in parallel — supports the identification assumption
  • After the cuts, treated groups’ taxable income rises ~10–15% relative to the control
  • People do respond to taxes — but the implied elasticity is only ≈ 0.2–0.25

Kansas bets on the wrong side

  • 2012: Kansas sharply cut taxes on top incomes
  • Top state rate: 6.45% → 4.9%; some business income taxed at zero
  • Gov. Sam Brownback: a “shot of adrenaline” — the cuts would pay for themselves
  • A state-level natural experiment in Laffer logic

What actually happened in Kansas

Kansas: reading the verdict

  • Tax revenue fell sharply relative to surrounding states (DeBacker et al. 2017)
  • Extra work effort came nowhere near offsetting the mechanical revenue loss
  • Kansas was not on the wrong side of the Laffer curve — the legislature rolled the cuts back in 2017

Bunching at kinks

Reading behavior straight off the tax code

  • Real tax schedules have kinks — points where the marginal incentive changes abruptly
  • If people respond to taxes, reported incomes should pile up (“bunch”) at the kinks
  • How much bunching → an estimate of the elasticity of taxable income
  • Best-known case: the EITC (Chetty, Friedman, and Saez 2013)

The EITC creates a kink

Why the kink matters

  • Earned Income Tax Credit: the largest cash anti-poverty program — roughly $70 billion per year
  • The credit rises with earnings, peaks, then phases out
  • One earnings level maximizes the refund — the first kink

  • In others (e.g., Kansas) the distribution is smooth — almost no bunching

Reading the maps

  • 1996: bunching is rare everywhere — just scattered hot spots (south Texas, Miami)
  • Watch the same map tick forward — 1999, 2002, 2005, 2008 — and the dark areas grow outward from those hot spots
  • By 2008 bunching spreads outward from early hot spots, like the diffusion of information
  • Moving to a high-bunching area raises your response; moving to a low-bunching area doesn’t — knowledge, not just compliance

What bunching does and doesn’t show

  • People respond to tax incentives — when they know about them
  • Audit studies: sharp bunching is partly misreported self-employment income, not just real work choices
  • Using employer-reported W-2 earnings, real labor supply responses exist but are smaller
  • Same lesson as Denmark and Kansas: behavioral responses are real but modest

So how high should top rates be?

Taxes and growth: the raw correlation

Source: Kuziemko, Norton, Saez, and Stantcheva (2015)

Correlation, not causation

  • The high-tax era (1933–80) had the fastest income growth; the low-tax era before 1933 had negative growth (−1.5%/yr)
  • Many things changed across these eras — wars, technology, education, demographics
  • The correlation cannot prove high taxes helped growth
  • But it is very hard to find evidence that high top rates crippled growth — the pattern runs the other way
  • Consistent with the small elasticities we just estimated

The Diamond–Saez case for a progressive tax

Source: Wall Street Journal (2012)

What Diamond and Saez argue

  • Your reading: Diamond and Saez (2011) — a Nobel laureate and the field’s leading empiricist
  • With elasticities around 0.25, they put the revenue-maximizing top federal rate at roughly 50–70%
  • We are not near the top of the Laffer curve, so raising top rates raises revenue

From journal page to campaign trail

Source: CNN (2019)

Two cautions

  • The 70% number jumped straight from this literature into politics
  • But the revenue-maximizing calculation puts zero weight on high-income families — an extreme assumption
  • And many Americans may care more about equality of opportunity than equality of outcomes
  • Economics narrows the debate; it does not end it

TopHat: What should the top rate be?

Given today’s evidence, what marginal tax rate would you set on income above $10 million?

  • A: under 30% · B: 30–50% · C: 50–70% · D: over 70%
  • There is no right answer — your vote is a value judgment about equity, informed by an elasticity from data

Recap

  • The U.S. leans unusually hard on income taxation; marginal ≠ average rates
  • Optimal taxes trade off equity (diminishing marginal utility) vs. efficiency (labor supply responses)
  • The Laffer curve caps rates: t^{*} = 1/(1+\varepsilon)
  • Denmark, Kansas, and bunching all say the elasticity is modest (~0.2–0.3) → revenue-maximizing top rates of 50–70% (Diamond and Saez 2011)
  • Where to land in that range is a value judgment

Tax policy II: savings, wealth, and behavioral public economics

Today’s question

Do people actually respond to taxes the way the textbook assumes — and what does it mean for policy if they don’t?

  • Last class: elasticities, the Laffer curve, optimal top rates — all built on people optimizing perfectly
  • Today we test that assumption with data — three times
  • Method spotlight: natural experiments from policy changes

Where we’re headed

  • Behavioral public economics: what if people are inattentive or uninformed?
  • Case 1: sales taxes and salience
  • Case 2: the EITC and the diffusion of knowledge
  • Case 3: retirement savings — defaults vs. subsidies in Denmark
  • Reading: Chetty, Friedman, Leth-Petersen, Nielsen, and Olsen (2014)

Behavioral public economics

The textbook assumption

  • Traditional model: everyone is fully aware of the taxes they face and optimizes perfectly in response
  • Every result from last class — elasticities, revenue-maximizing rates — leans on this assumption
  • Behavioral economics asks: is it true in practice?
  • If not, whole new policy tools open up

TopHat: Do you know your marginal tax rate?

Without looking it up: what is the marginal tax rate on the next dollar you (or your family) will earn this year?

  • A: I know it within a point or two
  • B: I could guess within 10 points
  • C: honestly, no idea
  • If you can’t state the rate, can you be optimizing against it?

Marginal rates are genuinely hard to know

Source: NBER Taxsim marginal tax rate calculator

Reading the zig-zag

  • Single parent, two kids, 2006: the marginal rate starts negative, around −25 to −40% (the EITC pays you to work)
  • It peaks near 51% at roughly $35–40K of earnings — as benefits phase out, this family faces a higher marginal rate than a CEO
  • A fully rational earner should know this entire zig-zag schedule
  • Would you?

The tax code doesn’t help

Source: Gruber (2016)

Two pages to 207

  • Instructions for the basic individual tax form: 2 pages in 1940 → 207 pages in 2013
  • Full awareness is an increasingly heroic assumption

Case 1: Are sales taxes salient?

The simplest tax there is

  • U.S. sales taxes are not included in posted prices: the shelf says $5, the register charges about $5.37
  • Rational benchmark: irrelevant — you know the tax exists and do the arithmetic in your head
  • Behavioral alternative: out of sight, out of mind
  • Chetty, Looney, and Kroft (2009) tested this in a real grocery store in Northern California

The experiment: put the tax on the shelf

← Original Tag ← Experimental Tag

Source: Chetty, Looney, and Kroft (2009)

The treatment, in words

  • New tags spell it out: $5.79 + sales tax = $6.22
  • About 1,000 cosmetics and hair-care products, ~3 weeks in 2006

Why hairbrushes?

  • The chain refused to let them touch big sellers — the managers believed the hypothesis and feared losing revenue
  • Impulse purchases are very price-elastic — good for detecting an effect if there is one
  • Built-in comparisons: untreated categories in the same aisle, plus similar control stores with no new tags

Results: a difference-in-differences (twice)

Change in units sold (treated − control categories) Estimate
Treatment store (DD) −2.14
Control stores (DD) 0.06
Triple difference (DDD) −2.20
  • Sales of tagged products fell about 8% (base ≈ 25 units)
  • Control stores show no differential trend — supports parallel trends

Nothing changed except visibility

  • No price changed. No tax changed. The tag just showed a tax that was already there
  • Making an ~8% tax visible cut purchases by ~8% — an implied elasticity of about 1, far from the “no effect” benchmark
  • Consumers were not fully accounting for the register tax
  • Salience — how visible a tax is — matters, not just its size

Confirmation from beer: excise taxes

Confirmation from beer: sales taxes

Same dollars, different visibility

  • State excise taxes on beer are baked into the posted price — consumption responds strongly when they change
  • State sales taxes are added at the register — consumption barely responds
  • Same dollars, different visibility, very different behavior

Case 2: The EITC and knowledge

Recap: responses spread across the map

Source: Chetty, Friedman, and Saez (2013)

Same federal credit, very different responses

  • Last class: sharp bunching at the refund-maximizing kink spread from south Texas across the U.S. between 1996 and 2008
  • The EITC is federal — incentives are identical everywhere
  • Leading explanation: knowledge about the credit diffuses like information, neighbor to neighbor

A sharp test: moving should be asymmetric

  • The knowledge story makes a very specific prediction about movers:
    • Move to a higher-bunching area → you learn → respond more
    • Move to a lower-bunching area → you can’t unlearn → respond the same
  • Compliance or enforcement stories predict symmetric changes
  • Asymmetry is the fingerprint of information

TopHat: What does the knowledge story predict?

A family moves from a high-bunching neighborhood to a low-bunching one. Their EITC response should…

  • A: fall — behavior follows the neighborhood
  • B: stay the same — knowledge, once gained, is kept
  • C: rise
  • Lock in your answer before the next slide

The movers event study

Source: Chetty, Friedman, and Saez (2013)

Asymmetry, exactly as predicted

  • Track the same filers before and after they move (tax data makes this easy)
  • Moves into the highest-bunching areas: bunching jumps sharply in the year of the move
  • Moves into the lowest-bunching areas: no decline at all
  • Exactly the asymmetry knowledge predicts

Learning about the credit pays

  • Compare changes in EITC refunds to changes in neighborhood bunching for movers
  • Moving to higher-bunching ZIPs: refunds rise steeply with local bunching (slope ≈ $60 per point)
  • Moving to lower-bunching ZIPs: essentially flat (slope ≈ $6); difference p < 0.0001
  • Learning raises refunds; “forgetting” doesn’t happen

Real responses, not just reporting

  • Caveat: audits show sharp bunching is partly misreported self-employment income — so test with W-2 wage earnings, reported directly by employers
  • And use a life event that switches the EITC on overnight: the birth of a first child

Knowledge shows up in W-2 earnings

  • Year before the birth: earnings distributions identical in high- and low-knowledge areas
  • Year of the birth: in high-bunching areas, wage earnings shift toward the EITC-maximizing range

  • The EITC does increase work among low-income families with kids — but its impact depends on knowledge of the program
  • Want a bigger labor supply effect? Raising awareness may beat raising the credit amount
  • Changing information or presentation ≈ a nudge: near-zero cost, real behavioral effects

Case 3: Retirement savings

The $100 billion question

  • Widespread concern that families save too little for retirement
  • The U.S. spends roughly $100 billion a year (in forgone tax revenue) subsidizing 401(k)s and IRAs
  • Put $10,000 in a 401(k) → deduct it from taxable income today; returns accumulate tax-free
  • Does this actually raise saving — or is there a cheaper tool?

The power of defaults (Madrian and Shea 2001)

  • Some employers auto-enroll workers in the 401(k); at others you must opt in via paperwork
  • The default changes no prices, no subsidies, no incentives — either way, the same plan is available
  • Traditional prediction: defaults are irrelevant
  • One large company changed its default — twice — creating a natural experiment

TopHat: Predict the effect

The company switches from opt-in to automatic enrollment at a 2% default contribution rate. Participation among new hires…

  • A: barely moves — the incentives are unchanged
  • B: rises by a few percentage points
  • C: jumps dramatically

What automatic enrollment did

Source: Madrian and Shea (2001)

Reading the three cohorts

  • Opt-in cohorts: ~20% early, grinding up to ~55% after 4 years
  • Auto-enrolled cohort: ~85% almost immediately
  • When the company ended the policy, new hires reverted to the slow path — the default itself did the work

People stick at the default rate, too

Source: Madrian and Shea (2001)

The default chooses the saving rate

  • 67% of auto-enrolled workers sit exactly at the 2% default, vs. 20% before and 9% after
  • The default isn’t just enrolling people — it is choosing their saving rate

The crowd-out worry

  • Before replacing $100B of subsidies with defaults, one big question: is the 401(k) money new saving?
  • Budget constraint: defaulted dollars must come from somewhere — less consumption, or less saving in other accounts
  • If people offset by draining their bank accounts, total saving is unchanged and the default achieved nothing
  • U.S. data can’t see all accounts. Denmark’s can

Why Denmark

  • Denmark levies a wealth tax → third-party records of every account for the entire population
  • Employer pensions work like 401(k) defaults; a 1999 reform changed the subsidy — two natural experiments in one dataset
  • Chetty, Friedman, Leth-Petersen, Nielsen, and Olsen (2014) — your reading

Natural experiment 1: switching firms

Job changes shock your default

  • Danish employers contribute to pensions automatically, and contribution rates differ a lot across firms
  • So switching jobs = a sudden, sharp change in your default saving rate — chosen by your employer, not you
  • Event study: workers who move to a firm contributing at least 3 points more of their pay
  • Do workers undo the change in their own accounts?

Taxable saving doesn’t budge either

Almost no offset

  • At the switch, employer pension contributions jump by 5.64% of income
  • Workers’ own pension contributions fall by just 0.56; taxable saving moves by 0.02
  • Roughly 90 cents of every defaulted krone is new total saving
  • Tracked over decades, these workers retire with substantially more wealth

Natural experiment 2: the 1999 subsidy cut

The policy change

  • Denmark subsidized individual pension contributions — the analog of the 401(k) tax break
  • 1999 reform: subsidy cut by 12 cents per krone of contributions — but only for taxpayers in the top bracket
  • Just below the bracket cutoff: nothing changed → a built-in comparison group
  • The price of retirement saving jumped for one group overnight

Contributions fell off a cliff…

  • Above the cutoff, pension contributions dropped sharply in 1999 and stayed down
  • Below the cutoff: no change at all
  • At first glance: a big win for the traditional price-response model

Where did the money go?

Source: Chetty, Friedman, Leth-Petersen, Nielsen, and Olsen (2014)

Where did the money go?

Source: Chetty, Friedman, Leth-Petersen, Nielsen, and Olsen (2014)

…but the money just changed accounts

  • The people who cut pension contributions raised saving in taxable accounts almost one-for-one
  • Marginal propensity to save in retirement accounts fell exactly when the propensity to save in taxable accounts rose
  • Total saving: essentially unchanged
  • The subsidy was moving money between accounts, not creating it

Active vs. passive savers

  • ~15% “active savers”: account for the entire response to the subsidy — but they only shuffle accounts, so net saving barely moves
  • ~85% “passive savers”: don’t respond to the subsidy at all
  • Passive savers are exactly the people defaults move — and for them, defaulted contributions become new wealth
  • The economy is mostly passive savers

Guess who responds to subsidies

Source: Chetty, Friedman, Leth-Petersen, Nielsen, and Olsen (2014)

Who are the active savers?

  • Responsiveness to the subsidy rises with education
  • The most responsive group of all: people trained in economics
  • Even the active responders mostly relabel saving rather than create it

Method spotlight: natural experiments

No one randomized anything

  • No researcher assigned workers to firms or taxpayers to brackets — policy and life created the variation
  • Firm switches: a sudden, dated change in defaults → event study around the move
  • The 1999 reform: a sudden price change for one group, with an untouched group right below the cutoff → comparison over time
  • Natural experiment: a policy change or event that mimics the random assignment of a real experiment

The recipe

  1. A sharp, dated change (reform, job switch, threshold)
  2. A treated group and a plausible comparison group
  3. Outcome data before and after — check that pre-trends are parallel
  • We’ve used this recipe before: opioid reformulation, NASCAR’s switch off leaded fuel, the Kansas tax cuts
  • Administrative big data is what makes step 3 credible

Behavioral economics widens the toolkit

  • Traditional levers: prices — tax rates and subsidies
  • Behavioral levers: salience, information, defaults
  • Often far cheaper — and, for passive majorities, far more effective
  • Match the tool to the population: prices move the active few; design moves the passive many

Recap

  • Salience: posting tax-inclusive prices cut purchases ~8% — taxes people don’t see, they ignore (Chetty, Looney, and Kroft 2009)
  • EITC: responses diffuse like knowledge — movers respond asymmetrically, and real W-2 responses appear where knowledge is high (Chetty, Friedman, and Saez 2013)
  • Denmark: defaults raise total saving (~90% pass-through); subsidies mostly shift accounts — 1 cent of new saving per $1
  • 85% passive vs. 15% active savers
  • Method: natural experiments from policy changes
