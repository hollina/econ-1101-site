Income and life expectancy

Chetty et al. (2016): the question and the data

  • Most common health measure: mortality — crude, but well measured for the whole population
  • Data: the entire U.S. population, 1999–2013 — 1.4 billion observations (Chetty et al., JAMA 2016)
  • Deaths from Social Security records; income from tax returns
  • Individuals ranked by household income percentile within birth cohort, gender, and tax year — the same rank logic as the Opportunity Atlas

What they estimate

  • Goal: expected age at death conditional on income percentile at age 40, adjusting for race and ethnicity
  • Period life expectancy: the length of life for a hypothetical person who faces this year’s mortality rates at every age — a snapshot, not a forecast
  • Three steps:
    1. Compute mortality rates by income rank and age
    2. Fit a survival model to extrapolate to old ages
    3. Adjust for racial differences in mortality

Reading the survival curves

  • Track the share of 40-year-old men still alive at each age
  • By age 76: only 52% of men at the 5th percentile are still alive, vs. 83% at the 95th percentile
  • Problem: the data stop at age 76 — but life expectancy needs the whole curve

Method spotlight: hazard models

  • Hazard rate m(a): the probability of dying at age a, given that you survived to age a
  • A survival curve strings hazards together: the share still alive at each age
  • A hazard model puts structure on how the hazard moves with age (or income, or treatment) — letting us predict beyond where the data end
  • Survival analysis is everywhere: patient survival, machine failure, loan default, unemployment spells

Gompertz (1825): mortality is exponential in age

  • Mortality rates grow exponentially with age — so log mortality is almost a perfectly straight line
  • Fits U.S. data remarkably well from 40 to 90 (small deviations after 90)
  • Fit the line through observed ages, then extrapolate past 76

Reading the Gompertz lines

  • Both percentiles: log mortality is close to linear in age
  • The p95 line starts lower but rises more steeply — rich and poor mortality rates converge at very old ages
  • Extrapolate each line to age 90; beyond 90, use national NCHS/SSA mortality estimates (assumed constant across income groups)

  • The bottom 1% are close to the average in Sudan or Pakistan

The Gift of a Lifetime

When did hospitals start saving lives?

  • Correlations today can’t tell us what access to modern medicine causes
  • Better idea: find a moment when some places suddenly got modern hospitals and others didn’t
  • That moment exists: the 1920s–1940s, exactly when hospitals were transforming from era-2 warehouses into era-3 centers of care
  • Today’s reading: Hollingsworth, Karbownik, Thomasson, and Wray (2024), “The Gift of a Lifetime,” American Economic Review

The Duke Endowment

  • 1924: James B. Duke commits roughly $40 million of his tobacco and hydropower fortune to a new philanthropy for the Carolinas
  • A core mission: hospitals — funds to build, expand, and modernize nonprofit community hospitals
  • Duke money pushed hospitals toward modern medicine: trained physicians, laboratories, better equipment and facilities
  • Some counties received Duke support; otherwise-similar counties did not

A natural experiment — and Project 3’s method

  • Compare infant mortality in counties with Duke-supported hospitals vs. counties without, before vs. after the money arrives
  • The change in treated counties minus the change in untreated counties: difference-in-differences
  • Differencing removes both fixed county differences and statewide trends
  • We’ll build this method formally later in the course — Project 3 is your first hands-on pass

What the paper finds

  • Infant mortality fell about 10% in supported counties, with larger declines for Black infants
  • Effects grew once sulfa drugs — the first effective antibacterials — arrived in the late 1930s: modern hospitals and new technology were complements
  • Children born in supported counties went on to live longer lives, decades later
  • Money spent when medicine works is extremely cost-effective

Recap

  • Two centuries of mortality decline: nutrition, then public health, then — only recently — medical care
  • Chetty et al. (2016): hazard models (Gompertz extrapolation) turn 1.4 billion records into life expectancy by income percentile
  • The gradient: 72.7 vs. 87.3 years for men — and the gap is growing
  • Across areas, low-income longevity tracks health behaviors, not health-care access — but those are correlations

Risk: a game of chance

Today is your lucky day

You have just won a $1 million lottery prize. Choose a payout:

  • Option A: Receive $1 million today
  • Option B: Trade it for a second lottery:
    • 10% chance of $5 million
    • 89% chance of $1 million
    • 1% chance of nothing

Expected value

  • The expected value of a gamble is its average payout: each possible payoff multiplied by its probability, summed up
  • A risk-neutral person uses expected value to compare gambles and to decide what a gamble is worth paying for
  • Quick check: a fair die pays you the number rolled in dollars. What’s the most a risk-neutral person pays to play?

Option B is clearly better… isn’t it?

Option Calculation Expected value
A 1.00 × $1M $1.00 million
B 0.10 × $5M + 0.89 × $1M + 0.01 × $0 $1.39 million
  • B is worth $390,000 more on average
  • Yet most people take A — that 1% chance of walking away with nothing looms very large

You can have preferences over risk

  • Risk neutral: only the expected value matters
  • Risk averse: between two gambles with the same expected value, you prefer the one with less variance
  • Risk loving: you prefer the one with more variance
  • Strong enough feelings about risk can make you give up expected value — like taking the certain $1 million
  • The same person can be risk loving over a $2 Powerball ticket and risk averse over their health — my poker-playing friends all carry very good health insurance

Risk aversion in plain terms

  • A dollar matters more when you have few of them — losing your last $10,000 hurts far more than gaining an extra $10,000 helps
  • So a certain outcome can feel better than a gamble with a higher average payout
  • Flip it around: people will pay a small certain amount to escape a small chance of a huge loss
  • That is exactly what insurance sells
  • Health is a bad lottery you are already holding a ticket to

From expected cost to a premium

  • Pure premium (actuarially fair premium): expected medical spending for people at her risk level = $6,000
  • Loading charge: what the insurer adds on top — administration, building the provider network, processing claims, marketing, profit
  • Here: 15% loading = $900, so her actual premium = $6,900
  • Would a rational person pay $6,900 to cover $6,000 of expected costs?

Why pay more than the actuarially fair premium?

  • Benefit 1 — reducing risk. Risk-averse people happily pay the loading charge to avoid huge year-to-year swings in what they can spend on everything else
  • Benefit 2 — enabling access. Insurance lets you get the $80,000 cancer treatment you could never pay for out of pocket in a very sick year
  • Both benefits are largest exactly when costs are rare but catastrophic

Spending is wildly concentrated

Source: Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker, 2016.

What that concentration means

  • The top 20% of users account for roughly 82% of medical spending; the entire bottom half accounts for only about 3%
  • Insurance pools that catastrophic risk across everyone

How the money flows

  • Enrollee pays a premium whether or not they use care — often split with an employer, which typically pays about 80% of it (e.g., you pay $1,500, your employer pays $6,000)
  • Insurer pays physicians, hospitals, and pharmacies when an enrollee receives care
  • Patient also pays cost sharing at the point of care — deductibles, copays
  • Medicare and Medicaid work similarly, with government as the payer

Method spotlight: adverse selection

Adverse selection, defined

  • Information asymmetry: you know far more about your own health — family history, lifestyle, that lump you haven’t mentioned — than any insurer can
  • Adverse selection: the people most eager to buy coverage are precisely the most expensive to cover
  • At $20,700 the insurer makes $14,700 on her and loses $9,300 on him — and only he wants to sign up
  • The pool that enrolls is sicker than the population average

Akerlof’s market for lemons

  • Used cars: sellers know which cars are lemons; buyers can’t tell (Akerlof 1970)
  • Buyers will only offer the price of an average car
  • Owners of good cars refuse that price and keep their cars — so the cars actually for sale get worse
  • Prices fall further, more good cars exit… the market can collapse entirely
  • Insurance is the same logic with the roles reversed: here the seller is the uninformed side

The death spiral, step by step

  1. One community-rated premium, no requirement to buy
  2. The healthiest look at $20,700 and walk away
  3. The remaining pool is sicker, so average cost per enrollee rises
  4. The insurer must raise the premium — and the next-healthiest group walks away
  5. Repeat until only the sickest remain and coverage becomes unaffordable: the market unravels

This actually happened

  • Harvard restructured its employee health benefits in the mid-1990s — employees now paid the full extra cost of the generous plan
  • Healthier employees switched out; the generous plan’s pool got sicker; premiums jumped; more people fled
  • Within three years the generous plan was gone — a documented death spiral (Cutler and Reber 1998)
  • Hendren’s estimates: the 2017 ACA-repeal bill would have effectively ended individual-market enrollment for families earning under $75,000

How insurers used to fight adverse selection

  • Deny: refuse applicants with pre-existing conditions
  • Exclusions: insure you, but not the condition you actually have
  • Retroactive denial: cancel the policy after the bills arrive
  • These protect the insurer by pushing the sickest people out of the market — the people who need insurance most

The ACA’s answer: a trade

  • 2014: everyone must buy insurance or pay a fine (individual mandate; low-income people were exempt); in exchange, insurers may no longer deny coverage for pre-existing conditions
  • Coverage reached about 91% of Americans — risk spread over a much broader pool
  • All plans must cover 10 essential health benefits — ending “skimpy” plans that had siphoned off the young and healthy
  • The mandate penalty ended in 2019 — adverse selection is reduced, not eliminated; the ~9% uninsured skew young and healthy

Employer pooling

Where working-age Americans get coverage

  • Most of the 271 million non-elderly Americans (2018) get insurance through an employer
  • The rest: public programs (Medicaid, Medicare, military), the individual market, or no coverage at all
  • Why does the employer market avoid the death spiral?

Why employer pooling works

  • The pool forms for a reason unrelated to health: people join a firm for the job, not because they feel sick
  • Insurers experience-rate the firm (sicker workforce → higher price to the company)…
  • …but community-rate within the firm: every employee pays the same, healthy or sick, young or old
  • At large firms adverse selection is minor — though young workers quietly subsidize older, sicker coworkers

  • For single coverage, the average employer pays about 81% of the premium; the employee covers the rest through payroll deductions
  • Government employers — like OSU — pay an even larger share, about 87%

Moral hazard

Demand for care slopes down

Source: Santerre and Neun, Health Economics, Exhibit 5.2.

Reading the demand curve

  • At $100 a visit you go twice; at $20 you go eight times
  • Insurance lowers the price you face at the point of care — so it changes how much care you use

Moral hazard, defined

  • Moral hazard: insurance against a loss weakens your incentive to prevent the loss or keep its cost down
  • Ex ante (before the event): skip the flu shot, eat the cheeseburgers, go hang gliding
  • Ex post (after the event): choose knee-replacement surgery when physical therapy would have done
  • Not fraud — just people responding rationally to prices

How price-sensitive is medical care?

  • The RAND Health Insurance Experiment randomized families into plans with different cost sharing
  • Overall: a 10% price decrease raises use of medical services by about 2%
  • But it varies: hospital care ~1.5%, primary care ~2%, mental health and nursing homes ~10%, choice of individual doctor ~30%
  • Higher-income groups are the least price-sensitive

Cost sharing: the fix, and its cost

  • Deductibles, copays, and coinsurance make patients face part of the price → trims low-value utilization
  • The catch: cost sharing re-introduces risk — the very thing insurance exists to remove
  • Every real plan is a compromise between full protection and moral hazard

Three limits of a pure private market

  1. Adverse selection: community-rated pools unravel into death spirals when the healthy can walk away
  2. Experience rating prices out the sick: insurers deny, exclude, and rescind exactly the people who need coverage most
  3. Moral hazard: generous coverage inflates spending, so full insurance is never offered
  • Together these explain both complicated insurance contracts and why every rich country’s government is deep in this market

What’s in a plan

We are collectively terrible at this

Source: Policygenius health insurance literacy survey.

Confidence outruns comprehension

  • 96% of surveyed Americans could not correctly define all four: deductible, coinsurance, copay, out-of-pocket maximum
  • In the next ten minutes, you join the 4%

Premium and network

  • Premium: the fixed amount you (and usually your employer) pay every month, care or no care — this is not cost sharing
  • Network: providers with whom your insurer has a negotiated rate (the allowed amount); go out of network and you pay more
  • Some plans require a referral from your primary care provider before seeing anyone else

Cost sharing: deductible, copay, coinsurance

  • Deductible: what you pay out of pocket each year before the insurer starts paying — cheaper plans have higher deductibles; preventive care is often exempt
  • Copay: a fixed dollar amount per service (e.g., $20 a visit), varying by service type
  • Coinsurance: the percentage of the bill you still pay after the deductible
  • All three are cost sharing — tools aimed at moral hazard

The out-of-pocket maximum

A year of bills under a $2,500 deductible, 25% coinsurance, and a $5,000 out-of-pocket limit.

Why the out-of-pocket max matters

  • The most you can spend on covered care in a year — deductible, copays, and coinsurance count; premiums do not
  • Fine print: a few plans do not count copays toward the maximum — check yours
  • This cap is what makes a plan insurance: it bounds your worst year

Actuarial value: plan generosity in one number

  • Actuarial value: the share of average covered costs a plan pays
  • ACA metal tiers: bronze 60%, silver 70%, gold 80%, platinum 90%
  • Many different deductible/coinsurance combinations reach the same actuarial value

Many designs, same actuarial value

Source: Kaiser Family Foundation, 2013.

Beyond the plan: FSAs, HRAs, and HSAs

  • Flexible Spending Account (FSA): you set aside tax-free dollars for out-of-pocket costs; you fund it, and unused funds do not roll over
  • Health Reimbursement Account (HRA): your employer funds and owns it; reimburses qualified medical expenses tax-free up to a fixed yearly amount, and funds can roll over
  • Health Savings Account (HSA): available only with a high-deductible health plan (HDHP); contributions are pre-tax, funds roll over, and after retirement they can be spent on non-medical expenses too
  • All of these are factored into a plan’s actuarial value

Recap

  • Risk aversion makes insurance worth more than expected costs — people rationally pay the loading charge
  • Pure premium + loading; experience rating vs. community rating
  • Adverse selection: information asymmetry → the sick select in, the healthy select out → death spiral (Akerlof 1970; Harvard, Cutler and Reber 1998)
  • Moral hazard is why plans make you share costs
  • Plan anatomy: premium, network, deductible, copay, coinsurance, out-of-pocket max

Health insurance II: the Oregon experiment and Medicaid

Today’s question

Does giving people health insurance actually make them healthier?

  • Last class: how insurance markets work (and fail)
  • Today: what insurance does — using the closest thing health economics has to a perfect experiment
  • Along the way, one result that surprised almost everyone

Where we’re headed

  • Why comparing insured to uninsured people is a trap
  • The Oregon Health Insurance Experiment: a Medicaid lottery
  • Effects on care use — including the emergency-department surprise
  • Effects on financial strain, depression, and clinical health
  • The Medicaid debate, and a third experiment run by the IRS

From RAND to Oregon

The question RAND left open

  • RAND compared more vs. less generous plans — everyone in the study had insurance
  • The big policy question is different: what happens when we cover people who have no insurance at all?
  • Does coverage mostly fuel wasteful spending, or does it improve health and financial security?
  • This is the question behind Medicaid expansion debates

Why not just compare insured to uninsured?

  • Uninsured adults differ from insured adults in many ways: income, age, jobs, health behaviors, existing illness
  • Remember adverse selection: who buys insurance depends on how sick they expect to be
  • Any raw comparison mixes the effect of insurance with the effects of everything else
  • Same problem as neighborhoods and schools earlier in the course: selection, not causation

TopHat: Uninsured adults are hospitalized less than Medicaid enrollees. Does Medicaid cause hospitalization?

  • A: Yes — the data show it directly
  • B: No — Medicaid enrollees are poorer and sicker to begin with, so the comparison is confounded
  • C: We can’t say anything at all from any data
  • Defend your answer with one word from earlier in the course

The ideal experiment

  • Randomly assign insurance to some people and not others, then compare outcomes
  • Randomization breaks the link between coverage and everything else about a person
  • Sounds impossible for health insurance — who would run it?
  • Amazingly, we have three such experiments: RAND, Oregon, and one run by the IRS

The Oregon lottery

2008: more applicants than money

  • Oregon had budget to expand Medicaid to some — but not all — low-income adults ages 19–64
  • Rather than first-come-first-served, the state allocated slots by randomized lottery
  • About 90,000 people signed up for the waiting list
  • A fiscal constraint accidentally created a landmark scientific experiment

The lottery design

  • Treatment group: about 30,000 people selected in the lottery and offered Medicaid
  • Control group: about 45,000 people who signed up but were not drawn
  • Studied in a series of papers by Baicker, Finkelstein, and co-authors (Finkelstein et al. 2012; Baicker et al. 2013)
  • Because selection was random, the two groups are identical on average — any later difference is caused by the lottery

Winning was not the same as enrolling

  • Winners still had to submit paperwork and meet the income rules
  • Only about 30% of lottery winners ended up enrolled in Medicaid
  • Winning raised the chance of having coverage by roughly 25 percentage points
  • So researchers compare everyone selected to everyone not selected — the intent-to-treat comparison — then scale up to get the effect of coverage itself

Method spotlight: why randomization works

  • With thousands of people, random draws balance everything — age, health, income, even things we can’t measure
  • Check: winners and losers looked nearly identical before the lottery
  • No self-selection into treatment → no adverse-selection bias
  • The lottery is the coin flip we could never ethically flip ourselves — a natural randomized experiment

The data

  • Medicaid administrative records: who actually enrolled
  • Hospital and emergency department records from Portland-area hospitals
  • Credit reports: medical debt, bills sent to collection
  • Mail surveys plus about 12,000 in-person interviews with blood tests and physical exams
  • Big data + an experiment: the best of both worlds

Effects on care use

Utilization: the first year

  • Medicaid increased use of nearly every kind of care (Finkelstein et al. 2012):
    • Outpatient visits up about 35%
    • Prescription drugs up about 15%
    • Hospital admissions up about 30%
  • Annual medical spending rose by roughly $778 per person — about 25%

Preventive care went up — mammograms roughly doubled

Source: Baicker et al. 2013

Insurance finds disease

Source: Baicker et al. 2013 — post-lottery diagnoses (Dx) and current medication (Rx)

Finding disease is the first step to treating it

  • Diabetes diagnoses jumped from ~1% to ~5%; diabetes medication use nearly doubled
  • You can’t treat a condition you don’t know you have

The ED finding: Medicaid increased ED use by about 40%

Source: Taubman et al. 2014

Why the ED result surprised people

  • The increase was roughly 0.4 extra ED visits per person over 18 months
  • Prediction was substitution: insured people would use primary care instead of the ER
  • Instead, primary care went up and ED use went up
  • Simple economics: Medicaid cut the out-of-pocket price of an ED visit to about zero — and demand curves slope down
  • Newly covered people used more of everything, including care that is expensive to provide

Financial strain

The clearest result: catastrophic costs nearly eliminated

Source: Baicker et al. 2013. Catastrophic = out-of-pocket costs above 30% of income.

Insurance is financial protection

  • Medical debt down about 13 percentage points; borrowing money or skipping other bills to pay medical bills down by more than half
  • This is exactly what insurance is for — recall the two benefits from last class: risk protection and access
  • Even if coverage never changed anyone’s blood pressure, it changed their balance sheets
  • Much of Medicaid’s value flows to recipients and to the providers and family members who were absorbing unpaid bills

Effects on health

Mental health: a large, real effect

  • Screening positive for depression fell from 30% to 21% — a drop of about 30% (Baicker et al. 2013)
  • One of the largest depression effects ever measured in a randomized setting
  • Plausible channels: actual treatment, plus relief from financial stress
  • Self-reported overall health also improved

Physical health: no detectable effect after ~2 years

Source: Baicker et al. 2013

Statistical power

  • Power: a study’s ability to detect an effect of a given size if it is really there
  • Only ~12,000 in-person exams, only ~2 years, and chronic disease moves slowly
  • The confidence intervals are wide — they include zero and they include clinically meaningful improvements
  • Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence
  • Also: the control group wasn’t untreated — ERs and charity care still existed

The Medicaid debate

Same study, dueling headlines

  • 2013, as states debated the ACA Medicaid expansion, both sides cited the same paper:
    • “Medicaid shows no improvement in physical health”
    • “Medicaid cuts depression by a third and ends catastrophic medical costs”
  • Both headlines are technically accurate
  • Knowing the methods — power, intent-to-treat, confidence intervals — is what lets you referee this fight

A third experiment — run by the IRS

  • In 2017 the IRS sent letters to 3.9 million households that had paid the ACA tax penalty (about $695 per adult) for being uninsured (Goldin et al. 2021)
  • Budget limits meant a random subset got no letter — another accidental randomized experiment
  • Outcomes tracked in administrative tax and death records — sample size ~50 times Oregon’s

The nudge: just the penalty amount, and where to sign up

Letters increased coverage by ~2 points — and it persisted…

…and reduced mortality: the first experimental evidence

Where the debate stands

  • Settled: coverage increases care use (including the ED), slashes financial hardship, and improves mental health
  • Was uncertain: effects on physical health — Oregon was too small to tell
  • Emerging: with far larger samples, coverage appears to reduce mortality among middle-aged adults
  • Keep it in perspective: these effects are probably too small to explain the large income differences in health we saw earlier in this topic — coverage alone won’t close that gap
  • The live policy question is no longer whether coverage helps, but how much it’s worth relative to what it costs

Recap

  • Comparing insured to uninsured people is confounded by selection — Oregon’s lottery solved that with randomization
  • Medicaid: care use up (~25% more spending, ED visits up 40%)
  • Financial hardship way down; depression down ~30%; clinical measures — null, but the study was underpowered
  • Same results fueled opposite headlines; methods literacy referees the debate
  • IRS experiment: at scale, coverage saves lives

Health insurance III: subsidies and policy design

Today’s question

Most low-income adults value health insurance at far less than it costs to provide. Should the government subsidize it anyway — and by how much?

  • Last two classes: what insurance does and why private markets for it struggle
  • Today: how much people actually value coverage — and how to design a subsidy
  • Reading: Finkelstein, Hendren, and Shepard (2019) on Massachusetts

Where we’re headed

  • Massachusetts’ 2006 reform — the dress rehearsal for the ACA
  • A clever natural experiment that reveals willingness to pay for insurance
  • The striking finding: willingness to pay sits far below cost
  • Why: uncompensated care and the value of cash
  • What that means for optimal subsidies — and heads up: Exam 2 is next class

Recap: the story so far

  • Insurance trades a small, certain premium for protection against rare, huge losses (adverse selection class)
  • Adverse selection can unravel private markets: the sick buy, the healthy leave, premiums spiral (adverse selection class)
  • Oregon: Medicaid coverage raised utilization, cut financial hardship, and reduced depression (Oregon experiment class)
  • Open question today: what is coverage worth to people — and who should pay for it?

Massachusetts: the ACA’s dress rehearsal

The 2006 Massachusetts reform

  • Bipartisan state law signed by Governor Mitt Romney in 2006 — nicknamed “Romneycare”
  • Goal: near-universal health insurance coverage in one state
  • Three tools: insurance market rules, an individual mandate, and income-based subsidies
  • It worked on its own terms: Massachusetts’ uninsured rate fell to the lowest in the nation (around 3%)

The three-legged stool

  1. Market rules — insurers must cover everyone at a common premium, healthy or sick (no pre-existing-condition denials)
  2. Individual mandate — a tax penalty for going uninsured, so healthy people stay in the pool
  3. Subsidies — sliding-scale help so the mandate is affordable at low incomes
  • Remove one leg and the stool tips: rules without a mandate invite adverse selection; a mandate without subsidies is unaffordable

Commonwealth Care

  • The subsidy leg: CommCare, a state exchange selling subsidized private plans
  • Eligible: adults below 300% of the federal poverty line without employer coverage or Medicaid
  • Coverage is free below 150% of the poverty line; above that, enrollees pay a premium that rises with income in steps
  • This program generates today’s data (Finkelstein, Hendren, and Shepard 2019)

From Boston to Washington

  • The 2010 Affordable Care Act copied the architecture nationally: exchanges + sliding-scale subsidies + mandate + market rules
  • Many of the same policy designers worked on both laws
  • So evidence from CommCare speaks directly to the ACA’s central design question:
  • How generous do subsidies have to be?

Measuring willingness to pay

Method spotlight: willingness to pay

  • Willingness to pay (WTP): the most a person would pay for something rather than go without it
  • A demand curve is just people lined up in order of WTP, highest first
  • If we watch how enrollment changes as the price changes, we can trace out the demand curve — and read off how much coverage is worth to people
  • That number, not utilization, is what cost-benefit analysis needs

The problem: prices don’t vary randomly

  • We can’t just ask people what they’d pay — talk is cheap
  • We need to see real choices at different prices
  • But people facing different premiums usually differ in other ways too (health, age, income)
  • We need price variation that is as good as random

The natural experiment in the subsidy schedule

CommCare’s monthly enrollee premium for the cheapest plan (2011):

Income (% of poverty line) Enrollee premium / month
Below 150% $0
150–200% $39
200–250% $77
250–300% $116
  • Earn $1 over a cutoff and your price jumps — compare people just above vs. just below: a regression discontinuity in prices (the Part 2 toolkit, back again)

TopHat: How many buy at a quarter of cost?

  • A. About 90%
  • B. About 70%
  • C. About 50%
  • D. About 20%

Demand falls fast

  • Enrollment drops about 25% for every $40 increase in the monthly premium (Finkelstein, Hendren, and Shepard 2019)
  • At premiums equal to ~25% of insurers’ average cost, at most half of eligible adults buy coverage
  • Even at premiums of just 10% of average cost, at least 20% remain uninsured

The headline finding

  • For at least 70% of this low-income population, willingness to pay is less than half of the cost they would impose on the insurer
  • Not a rounding error — a chasm between value and cost
  • Nobody would design a market expecting people to buy a product they value at less than half its price
  • This gap is the central puzzle of today’s class

Adverse selection along the demand curve

Who buys when the price is high?

  • The demand curve ranks people by WTP — and for insurance, WTP tracks expected sickness
  • So the people willing to pay the most are also the costliest to cover
  • As the premium falls and enrollment grows, each newly attracted enrollee is healthier and cheaper than those already in
  • Demand and cost curves are linked — that is what makes insurance markets special

Marginal vs. average cost

  • Average cost: insurer spending per enrollee among those currently enrolled
  • Marginal cost: spending on the next person who would enroll if the price fell a little
  • FHS finding: marginal enrollees always cost less than average enrollees — at every price
  • This is adverse selection along the demand curve (Einav and Finkelstein 2011)

TopHat: Premiums go up. Who leaves first?

CommCare raises its premiums by $40 a month. Which enrollees drop coverage first?

  • A. The sickest, most expensive enrollees
  • B. The healthiest, cheapest enrollees
  • C. A random mix of enrollees

Why a private market unravels here

  • A private insurer must charge at least average cost to break even
  • But almost everyone’s WTP is far below average cost
  • Unsubsidized equilibrium: premiums high, pool small and sick — the death-spiral logic from the adverse selection class, now measured in real data
  • Subsidies do double duty: they raise coverage and pull in healthy enrollees who improve the risk pool

Why is willingness to pay so low?

The puzzle

  • The adverse selection class said risk-averse people should pay more than the actuarially fair premium — that’s why insurance exists
  • Yet low-income adults in Massachusetts won’t pay even half of cost
  • Are they making a mistake?
  • Or is something else going on?

  • B. About $5,000

Uncompensated care: implicit insurance

  • Hospitals must treat emergencies regardless of insurance; charity care and unpaid bills absorb much of the rest
  • The uninsured end up paying only about 20 cents of every dollar of care they receive
  • So the uninsured already hold implicit insurance — formal coverage partly pays for care someone else was already covering
  • That mechanically drags down willingness to pay for a formal policy

Cash is scarce at low incomes

  • A dollar is worth more when you have very few of them
  • Premiums compete with rent and groceries today; insurance pays off only in rare bad states of the world
  • For a family at 200% of the poverty line, $77/month is a real sacrifice
  • Low WTP is not necessarily a mistake — it can be a rational response to a tight budget plus an implicit safety net

Uncompensated care is not free

  • Somebody pays the other 80 cents: hospitals, state programs, and ultimately taxpayers and other patients
  • When a person gains coverage, part of the insurer’s cost simply replaces uncompensated care that others were already funding
  • Covering the uninsured therefore creates an external benefit to the rest of society
  • That externality is the key to thinking about the subsidy

Designing the subsidy

The optimal subsidy logic

  • Subsidize coverage as long as its social value exceeds its social cost
  • Social value = enrollee’s own WTP + uncompensated care avoided + a healthier risk pool (marginal enrollees are cheap)
  • WTP alone would justify only small subsidies — the externalities close much of the gap
  • The gap that remains is a genuine policy judgment: how much do we value coverage people wouldn’t buy themselves?

What the estimates imply

  • To cover most eligible low-income adults, premiums must be pushed to roughly 10% of cost or less — subsidies of about 90%
  • Modest subsidies buy only modest coverage: there is no cheap path to near-universal coverage
  • This is exactly why ACA subsidies are largest at the bottom of the income distribution and phase out as income rises
  • Massachusetts got near-universal coverage by paying for it

What about the mandate?

  • The stick instead of the carrot: Massachusetts, then the ACA, penalized going uninsured (in 2017, at least $695 per adult)
  • The federal penalty was set to zero starting in 2019
  • Nudges matter too: IRS letters reminding households of the penalty measurably raised coverage — and even reduced mortality (Goldin, Lurie, and McCubbin 2021)
  • But when WTP is far below cost, penalties on low-income families are hard to justify and hard to enforce — subsidies do the heavy lifting

The stakes in 2017: what the evidence implied

  • The FHS findings entered the national debate over repealing the ACA
  • The 2017 repeal bill offered flat, much smaller subsidies
  • Hendren’s estimate: enrollment would effectively collapse for families making under $75,000 — nearly the whole subsidized market

Recap

  • Massachusetts 2006 = the ACA prototype: market rules + mandate + subsidies
  • Premium discontinuities trace out demand: enrollment falls ~25% per $40; WTP is below half of cost for at least 70% of eligible adults
  • Marginal enrollees are cheaper than average — adverse selection along the demand curve
  • WTP is low because the uninsured pay only ~20 cents per dollar of care (uncompensated care) and cash is scarce
  • Near-universal coverage requires ~90% subsidies — the externality argument helps justify them

The opioid crisis and drug policy

Why do roughly 100,000 Americans a year die of drug overdoses — and which policies actually reduce deaths?

Why fentanyl changed everything

A lethal dose: about 2 milligrams

Source: DEA photo.

Fentanyl, in three facts

  • Roughly 50× more potent than heroin; a lethal dose is ~2 mg
  • Cheap to make in a lab, easy to smuggle, easy to mix into other drugs
  • Users often don’t know fentanyl is in what they bought

Three waves of the crisis

One crisis, three waves

Naming the waves

  • Wave 1 (late 1990s–2010): prescription opioids
  • Wave 2 (~2010–2013): heroin
  • Wave 3 (2013–): fentanyl and synthetic opioids

Wave 1: America’s prescribing habit

17.4 days per resident per year — ~40% above Canada

Source: International Narcotics Control Board, via New York Times.

Why did this happen? No single cause

  • Drug companies: unprecedented marketing of a scheduled substance to physicians; claims that addiction risk was low
  • Insurers: pills reimbursed more readily than physical therapy
  • Drug trafficking organizations: e.g., Xalisco heroin cells delivering like pizza (Quinones, Dreamland)
  • Culture of pain treatment: pain as “the fifth vital sign”
  • Multiple causes — but can we isolate any one of them credibly?

A natural experiment: triplicate states

Method spotlight: natural experiments

  • A natural experiment: a policy or event — not designed as research — that splits otherwise-similar groups into treated and untreated
  • We compare outcomes across groups the event created, just like a randomized experiment
  • The key assumption: absent the event, the groups would have moved in parallel
  • Today’s example is one of the cleanest in health economics

OxyContin, 1996

  • Purdue Pharma launches OxyContin: a slow-release, high-dose oxycodone pill
  • Marketed aggressively to physicians as safe for everyday chronic pain with low addiction risk
  • Sales reps, speaker fees, and ad campaigns on a scale never before seen for a controlled substance
  • But Purdue did not market equally hard everywhere…

Five states were different

  • California, Idaho, Illinois, New York, Texas had “triplicate prescription” programs: Schedule II drugs required a special three-copy form, with one copy going to the state
  • Extra hassle + state surveillance → doctors prescribed fewer Schedule II drugs
  • Purdue’s own internal documents: triplicate states were a weak market, so focus marketing on the other 45 states
  • Nature just ran an experiment: 5 low-exposure states vs. 45 high-exposure states (Alpert et al. 2022)

What happened after 1996

Overdose death rates, triplicate vs. non-triplicate states, 1983–2017. Source: Alpert, Evans, Lieber, and Powell (2022).

Two decades later, the gap is still there

  • Before 1996: triplicate states had slightly higher overdose deaths, moving in parallel with the rest
  • After launch: non-triplicate states diverge — and keep diverging for 20+ years, right through the heroin and fentanyl waves
  • Marketing exposure in the 1990s predicts fentanyl deaths in 2017
  • Lesson 1: the crisis has supply-side origins — it wasn’t just pre-existing demand
  • Lesson 2: addiction makes early exposure extraordinarily persistent

Demand-side policies

The policy menu

  • Cut new addictions: initial prescription limits
  • Make pills harder to misuse: abuse-deterrent formulations
  • Catch misuse and doctor shopping: prescription drug monitoring programs (PDMPs)
  • (Plus: naloxone access, treatment expansion, harm reduction — a full course on their own)
  • For each: what does credible evidence say?

Do prescription limits work?

  • Limits do bind: long first prescriptions largely disappear
  • But effects on opioid initiation are modest — most first scripts were already short

Days supplied in initial opioid prescriptions, states with vs. without a 7-day limit. Source: Sacks, Hollingsworth, Nguyen, and Simon (2021).

Abuse-deterrent formulations

  • Many users crushed OxyContin to snort or inject it — defeating the slow release and getting the full dose at once
  • In 2010, Purdue reformulated OxyContin to be nearly impossible to crush
  • On paper: the flagship abuse-deterrent formulation (ADF)
  • What would an economist predict happens next?

The reformulation backfired

  • Users didn’t stop using — they switched to heroin: cheaper per dose and suddenly more attractive (Alpert et al. 2018; Evans et al.
  • Wave 2’s timing lines up almost exactly with the 2010 reformulation
  • Total overdose deaths did not fall — the composition shifted to a deadlier drug
  • General lesson: squeezing one drug’s supply while demand is unchanged causes substitution

Supply-side evidence: Australia

Is there a role for supply-side reduction?

  • Skeptic’s case: demand from addiction is highly inelastic — interdiction just raises prices and enriches traffickers
  • Believer’s case: higher prices and scarcity must reduce use somewhat — the question is how much, and what replaces it
  • Hard to test: U.S. enforcement changes are gradual and endogenous
  • We need a big, sharp, unexpected supply shock… enter Australia

Australia’s 1990s heroin epidemic

  • Over 75% heroin, widespread methadone treatment participation
  • By 1999, opioid deaths had eclipsed traffic fatalities in NSW
  • Growth rate over the 1990s looks almost exactly like the recent U.S. epidemic — a decade earlier and an ocean away

What happened to the market

  • Purity-adjusted heroin price rose ~400% in late 2000/early 2001
  • Attributed to interdiction: supply routes dismantled, record seizures

The long run: deaths stayed low

Mortality by underlying cause and opioid offense rates, NSW, 1995–2008. Source: Moore and Schnepel (2021).

A permanent decline

  • Opioid deaths fell immediately and never rebounded — still far below pre-shortage levels seven years later
  • Opioid offenses show the same permanent drop

Substitution happened here too

Cocaine and amphetamine offense rates, NSW, 1995–2008. Source: Moore and Schnepel (2021).

Substitution — but to safer drugs

  • Cocaine use spiked briefly, then faded
  • Amphetamine use rose and persisted — substitution is real
  • Crucial difference from the U.S.: the substitutes were less lethal than injected heroin, not more

What about crime?

Property and violent offense rates, NSW, 1995–2008. Source: Moore and Schnepel (2021).

Crime fell too

  • A brief spike as the market convulsed — then property crime fell by more than half over the following years
  • Consistent with less income-generating crime to fund heroin use

Why did Australia’s supply shock “work”?

  • The shock was large, sudden, durable, and nationwide — not a local squeeze users could route around
  • Available substitutes (amphetamines) were less deadly than heroin
  • Treatment (methadone maintenance) was widely available to absorb demand
  • Contrast the U.S.: cracking down on pills pushed users toward heroin and fentanyl — substitutes that were cheaper and deadlier
  • Supply-side policy can save lives — but only paired with the right substitution environment

Deaths of despair

The bigger backdrop: rising midlife mortality

  • Case and Deaton (2015): all-cause mortality rising since the late 1990s for middle-aged white Americans without a college degree — unique among rich countries
  • Driven by drugs, alcohol-related liver disease, and suicide — together, “deaths of despair”
  • Their story: decades of declining economic prospects for less-educated workers eroded health, family, and community

Is the economy the root cause?

  • Opioid abuse is countercyclical: as a county’s unemployment rate rises 1 point, opioid death rates rise ~3.6% (Hollingsworth, Ruhm, and Simon 2017)
  • But changing economic conditions explain only a small share of the overall rise in drug deaths (Ruhm 2019)
  • Despair may explain who is most vulnerable; supply — marketing, pills, heroin, fentanyl — explains the timing and scale
  • Both matter: demand-side despair met an unprecedented supply shock

Putting supply and demand together

  • Triplicate states: supply/marketing exposure created the crisis and its geography
  • Reformulation and Rx crackdowns: demand persisted → substitution to deadlier drugs
  • Australia: supply reduction saved lives when substitutes were safer and treatment was available
  • Serious policy needs both blades of the scissors: limit supply of the deadliest drugs and treat the underlying addiction

Recap

  • Three waves: prescription opioids → heroin (2010) → fentanyl (2013); peak above 100,000 deaths/year
  • Triplicate states are a natural experiment: less OxyContin marketing in 1996 → fewer overdose deaths for 20+ years
  • Demand-side: must-access PDMPs help; ADF reformulation backfired via substitution
  • Australia’s heroin shortage: a supply shock that cut deaths permanently — substitutes and treatment made the difference
  • Deaths of despair: demand-side vulnerability, supply-side spark
